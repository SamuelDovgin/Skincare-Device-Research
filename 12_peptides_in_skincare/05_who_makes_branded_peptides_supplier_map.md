# Who Actually Makes Skincare Peptides? Supplier, Trademark, and Licensing Map

*Compiled 2026-07-13. Current company/product pages were checked 2026-07-13. This is market and formulation research, not legal or medical advice. Confidence legend: verified primary source / supplier marketing claim / inference.*

## 0. Bottom line

**Yes—the story you heard is substantially true, but there is not one company making all cosmetic peptides.** A relatively small group of specialist ingredient houses develops, manufactures, documents, and sells many of the named peptide technologies that finished skincare brands put into serums and creams.

The most likely companies behind the story are:

1. **Sederma, now presented as Croda Beauty Actives** — the company behind the **Matrixyl** family. Croda says Matrixyl is used in more than 1,500 skincare products. [[1]](https://www.crodabeauty.com/en-gb/capabilities/actives)
2. **Lipotec, part of Lubrizol** — the company behind **Argireline** and several related peptide solutions. Lubrizol calls itself the sole global supplier and trademark owner of authentic Argireline and says the ingredient appears in more than 3,000 products; those counts are supplier claims based on commercial databases, not independent efficacy evidence. [[2]](https://www.lubrizol.com/company/insights/2026/02/not-all-peptides-are-created-equal)
3. **Caregen** — a large Korean peptide platform company. Its current site claims more than 1,800 peptide assets; a preserved 2021 BASF/Caregen announcement verifies a global exclusive BASF supply agreement for four Caregen cosmetic peptides and described Caregen as then holding more than 360 peptide-substance patents. [[3]](https://www.caregen.co.kr/)[[4]](source_docs/basf-caregen-exclusive-peptide-supply-agreement-2021.pdf)
4. **dsm-firmenich / legacy Pentapharm** — the supplier behind the **SYN** peptide family, including SYN-AKE and SYN-COLL. [[5]](https://www.dsm-firmenich.com/en/businesses/perfumery-beauty/beauty-care/products/syn-ake.html)[[6]](https://www.dsm-firmenich.com/en/businesses/perfumery-beauty/beauty-care/products/syn-coll.html)

“Companies pay to use them” is directionally right, but the public evidence usually supports a **raw-material purchase and trademarked-ingredient relationship**, not a visible per-bottle royalty. A brand or its contract manufacturer buys a commercial peptide solution/premix, formulates it into the finished product, and may be allowed to feature the supplier’s trademark and substantiation package. Separate patent licences, trademark permissions, exclusivity, minimum orders, or royalties may exist, but those contracts are usually private. Do not assume a separate licensing fee unless the parties disclose one.

The clearest public example is The Ordinary’s Multi-Peptide + HA Serum: its own page says the finished serum combines **SYN-AKE** from dsm-firmenich/DSM, **Matrixyl 3000 and Matrixyl Synthe’6** from Sederma, and **ARGIRELOX** from Lipotec. One consumer “multi-peptide” serum therefore combines technologies from at least three upstream suppliers. [[7]](https://theordinary.com/en-us/multi-peptide-ha-serum-100613.html)

## 1. Why this page exists

The other pages in this topic rank peptide biology and human evidence. They do not answer the commercial question: **who made the active, what did the finished brand buy, and what does a trademark or headline percentage actually prove?**

That distinction matters because five things are often collapsed into one word:

- the **peptide molecule or sequence**;
- its generic **INCI ingredient name**;
- the supplier’s **commercial grade**—often a dilute solution containing carriers, solubilizers, stabilizers, and preservative;
- the supplier’s **trademark**, such as Matrixyl or Argireline;
- the consumer brand’s **finished formula**, which can mix several supplier technologies with humectants and other actives.

A clinical result for one supplier grade or one finished formula does not automatically validate every material with a matching INCI name.

## 2. Evidence map

| Question | Best evidence found | Finding | Confidence / limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is one upstream company behind many familiar peptide serums? | Croda and Lubrizol portfolio pages | Yes, especially within named families: Sederma/Croda for Matrixyl and Lipotec/Lubrizol for Argireline | **Verified company identity; product-count figures are supplier/database claims** |
| Is there literally one company behind most cosmetic peptides? | Croda, Lubrizol, BASF/Caregen, and dsm-firmenich primary pages | No. Several major houses compete, and generic/custom synthesis also exists | **Verified** |
| Do suppliers sell a finished pure peptide powder straight into the serum? | Supplier product INCI and development formulas | Often no. The sellable material is a ready-to-formulate liquid or delivery system containing a small peptide fraction plus a carrier | **Verified for the named examples** |
| Do finished brands combine supplier technologies? | The Ordinary Multi-Peptide + HA product page | Yes: one formula expressly identifies SYN, two Matrixyl grades, and ARGIRELOX | **Verified brand disclosure** |
| Is “10% Matrixyl” or “10% Argireline” necessarily 10% pure peptide? | Supplier INCI/use-level pages and historical patents | No. The percentage can refer to the supplier solution or technology, whose pure peptide fraction is much smaller | **Verified principle; exact current grade concentration may be confidential** |

US retail labels use common/usual ingredient names; trade names are not acceptable substitutes | **Verified for US labelling** [[8]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/resources-industry-cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredient-suppliers-fact-sheet) |

## 3. The upstream companies

### 3.1 Sederma / Croda Beauty Actives: the Matrixyl house

Croda’s history page says its actives business—formerly Sederma—introduced palmitoylated cosmetic peptides in 1990, launched the original Matrixyl in 2000, Matrixyl 3000 in 2002, Matrixyl Synthe’6 in 2010, and Matrixyl Morphomics in 2017. [[9]](https://www.crodabeauty.com/en-gb/capabilities/peptides/timeline)

These are a **family of different commercial ingredients**, not one interchangeable peptide:

| Supplier name | Active peptide name(s) shown by Croda | What the commercial material contains |
|---|---|---|
| **Matrixyl** | Palmitoyl Pentapeptide-4 | Glycerin, water, butylene glycol, carbomer, polysorbate 20, and the peptide [[10]](https://www.crodabeauty.com/en-gb/products/product/5598-matrixyl) |
| **Matrixyl 3000** | Palmitoyl Tripeptide-1 + Palmitoyl Tetrapeptide-7 | Glycerin, water, butylene glycol, carbomer, polysorbate 20, and both peptides; Croda also offers other carrier grades [[11]](https://www.crodabeauty.com/en-gb/products/product/3007-matrixyl_1_3000) |
| **Matrixyl Synthe’6** | Palmitoyl Tripeptide-38 | Glycerin, water, hydroxypropyl cyclodextrin, and the peptide [[12]](https://www.crodabeauty.com/en-gb/products/product/3046-matrixyl_1_synthe_1_6) |
| **Matrixyl Morphomics** | N-Prolyl Palmitoyl Tripeptide-56 Acetate | Water and the peptide ingredient [[13]](https://www.crodabeauty.com/en-gb/products/product/3085-matrixyl_1_morphomics) |

Croda’s own 2025 development formula is especially revealing: the formulator adds **3% Matrixyl 3000 commercial material**, and the row lists the two peptides inside that material. It does not say the finished product contains 3% pure peptide. [[14]](source_docs/croda-matrixyl3000-development-formulation-2025.pdf)

**Conclusion:** if the story was about one supplier behind a large share of famous anti-wrinkle peptide blends, **Sederma/Croda is probably the name you heard**.

### 3.2 Lipotec / Lubrizol: the Argireline house

Lubrizol says it acquired Lipotec’s active-ingredients business around 2012. [[15]](https://www.lubrizol.com/company/insights/2022/10/argireline-amplified-peptide-recognized-at-first-alle-awards) Its current classic Argireline product page identifies the sellable material as **water, Acetyl Hexapeptide-8, and caprylyl glycol**, with a recommended use level of **2–10%** of that solution. [[16]](https://espanol.lubrizol.com/personal-care/products/product-finder/products-data/argireline-peptide-solution-c)

That is the same upstream pattern as Matrixyl:

- Lipotec/Lubrizol owns the Argireline brand and supplies commercial grades.
- A finished brand buys and incorporates a solution or delivery system.
- The consumer label lists **Acetyl Hexapeptide-8**, not “Argireline” in place of the INCI name.
- The brand may separately use Argireline in product marketing and acknowledge the trademark owner.

Lubrizol now sells more than one delivery format. For example, Argireline YOUth is an oil-compatible inverse-micelle system whose INCI includes emollient, emulsifier, water, and Acetyl Hexapeptide-8; its recommended use level is 0.8–2% of that commercial material. [[17]](https://www.lubrizol.com/Personal-Care/Products/Product-Finder/Products-Data/Argireline-YOUth-peptide)

### 3.3 Caregen + BASF: the platform and exclusive-supply model

Caregen is the strongest match for the idea of a company that has designed **a very large library of proprietary peptides** and then works through commercial partnerships. Its current company site claims more than 1,800 peptide assets and explicitly links to partnership/licensing activity. Treat those totals as company self-report. [[3]](https://www.caregen.co.kr/)

The more concrete primary record is the 2021 joint announcement preserved locally: BASF signed a **global, exclusive supply agreement for four Caregen cosmetic peptides**. BASF said it selected candidates from Caregen’s “huge portfolio” and adapted them to cosmetic-industry standards. [[4]](source_docs/basf-caregen-exclusive-peptide-supply-agreement-2021.pdf)

This is a different but related layer of the model:

1. Caregen discovers/designs and produces peptide technology.
2. BASF receives exclusive global supply rights for selected cosmetic peptides.
3. BASF turns them into a market-ready ingredient range and sells to cosmetic formulators.
4. Finished brands buy from the cosmetic-ingredient channel rather than necessarily dealing with Caregen directly.

BASF’s current peptide overview lists both defined synthetic peptides and blends/delivery systems, including the Peptovitae range and Replexium, a blend of two acetylated tetrapeptides. [[18]](https://care360.basf.com/emea/en/industries/personal-care/concepts-trends/concepts/peptides)

### 3.4 dsm-firmenich / Pentapharm: the SYN family

dsm-firmenich’s SYN-AKE page identifies the commercial active as a glycerin/water solution of **Dipeptide Diaminobutyroyl Benzylamide Diacetate** and offers samples only to business users. [[5]](https://www.dsm-firmenich.com/en/businesses/perfumery-beauty/beauty-care/products/syn-ake.html) Its SYN-COLL page identifies a newer commercial **Palmitoyl Tripeptide-5** grade. [[6]](https://www.dsm-firmenich.com/en/businesses/perfumery-beauty/beauty-care/products/syn-coll.html)

The SYN family shows why a front-label nickname is not enough: SYN-AKE and SYN-COLL are different molecules, with different proposed targets and evidence. The shared “SYN” branding identifies a supplier family, not a common mechanism.

### 3.5 Generic and custom manufacturers

Peptides such as Copper Tripeptide-1, Acetyl Hexapeptide-8, Palmitoyl Tripeptide-1, and Palmitoyl Pentapeptide-4 can also be synthesized by manufacturers other than the famous trademark owner. A matching INCI name may identify the nominal molecule, but it does not prove:

- the same purity, salt/counterion, stereochemistry, impurities, or batch controls;
- the same carrier and preservation system;
- the same stability in the finished formula;
- authorization to use the supplier trademark;
- applicability of the original supplier’s clinical or safety package.

Lubrizol’s 2026 article explicitly distinguishes authentic Argireline from “copycat” materials with matching ingredient names. That is a supplier-authored authenticity claim, but it correctly highlights that **same INCI is not proof of same commercial grade or same evidence package**. [[2]](https://www.lubrizol.com/company/insights/2026/02/not-all-peptides-are-created-equal)

## 4. What the brand is actually paying for

The commercial package can include more than the peptide sequence:

| Layer | What the ingredient house may provide | What remains the finished brand’s job |
|---|---|---|
| Molecule | Sequence design, synthesis, purification, identity testing | Select an appropriate active for the claim |
| Commercial grade | Water/oil carrier, solubilization, encapsulation, preservation, pH window, use level | Incorporate it without destabilizing the formula |
| Documentation | INCI, specification, certificate of analysis, safety data, regulatory status, formulation guide | Verify the received lot and retain product records |
| Substantiation | In-vitro, ex-vivo, supplier-run human tests, before/after assets | Substantiate the **finished product’s** claims; do not overextend raw-ingredient data |
| IP/branding | Trademark, patent portfolio, logo/name guidelines, sometimes exclusivity | Use the name lawfully and give any required attribution |
| Distribution | Samples, regional distributor, technical support, reference formulas | Purchase at commercial scale or through a contract manufacturer |

The public pages do **not** establish a universal fee structure. In many ingredient businesses, access is monetized through the price of the proprietary raw material and the technical/marketing package. An additional trademark licence, patent licence, territory restriction, exclusivity payment, or royalty is possible, but it must be verified deal by deal.

## 5. Hero ingredient versus supplier blend: a finished-brand case study

The Ordinary’s current pages show all three common patterns:

| Finished product | Brand presentation | Upstream trail visible on official page | What it proves |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Matrixyl 10% + HA** | Matrixyl is the hero technology | Two Matrixyl generations; trademark attributed to Sederma | A finished brand can center a product on a supplier family without being affiliated with the supplier [[19]](https://theordinary.com/en-us/matrixyl-10-ha-serum-100431.html) |
| **Argireline Solution 10%** | Argireline is the hero technology | Argireline trademark attributed to Lipotec | A supplier solution can become the name and main marketing story of the finished serum [[20]](https://theordinary.com/en-us/argireline-solution-10-serum-100403.html) |
| **Multi-Peptide + HA** | Multi-technology blend | SYN-AKE, Matrixyl Synthe’6, Matrixyl 3000, ARGIRELOX, and another peptide technology; owners identified as DSM, Sederma, and Lipotec | One finished formula can combine multiple upstream suppliers [[7]](https://theordinary.com/en-us/multi-peptide-ha-serum-100613.html) |

The trademark acknowledgements are evidence of ownership and the brand’s claimed use of those technologies. They do **not** disclose purchase price, royalty terms, minimum use level, the amount of pure peptide, or whether the finished blend has a matched-vehicle clinical trial.

## 6. The percentage trap

### 6.1 Supplier-material percent is not pure-peptide percent

A supplier may recommend adding 2%, 3%, 5%, or 10% of its **commercial solution**. That solution may itself be mostly water, glycerin, glycol, oil, cyclodextrin, emulsifier, or preservative.

Two primary examples make the scale clear:

- Croda’s development formula uses **3.00% Matrixyl 3000 material**, while the material’s INCI includes five carrier/formulation ingredients plus two peptides. [[14]](source_docs/croda-matrixyl3000-development-formulation-2025.pdf)
- Lubrizol recommends **2–10% Argireline solution C**, whose disclosed INCI is water, Acetyl Hexapeptide-8, and caprylyl glycol. [[16]](https://espanol.lubrizol.com/personal-care/products/product-finder/products-data/argireline-peptide-solution-c)

Therefore, a product name such as “10% Matrixyl” or “Argireline Solution 10%” should be read as a percentage of a **technology/solution unless the brand explicitly says pure peptide and provides the calculation**.

### 6.2 Historical concentration examples

A Procter & Gamble patent describes the commercial original Matrixyl composition as containing **100 ppm palmitoyl-KTTKS**. [[21]](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6492326B1/en) If that historical grade were added at 3% of a finished formula, the arithmetic would be:

`100 ppm × 0.03 = 3 ppm pure pal-KTTKS in the finished product`

That 3 ppm endpoint is the concentration used in the landmark pal-KTTKS clinical trial described in the ranked-evidence page. This calculation is useful for scale, but it should not be assumed for every current Matrixyl grade or every finished product.

Likewise, a patent describing a commercial Argireline solution states that the solution contained about **0.05% peptide powder in water plus preservative**. [[22]](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2016154020A1/en) Under that described grade, adding 10% solution would yield about:

`0.05% × 0.10 = 0.005% pure peptide = 50 ppm`

Current supplier grades can change, so the exact active fraction requires the current technical data sheet or direct supplier confirmation. The point is not that every Argireline serum contains exactly 50 ppm; it is that **10% supplier solution and 10% pure peptide differ by orders of magnitude**.

## 7. How to audit a peptide product

### Stronger traceability

- The brand names the exact supplier trademark and its owner.
- The full INCI contains the expected peptide **and** carrier pattern for that supplier grade.
- The brand distinguishes “10% peptide complex/solution” from pure-peptide concentration.
- The exact finished formula—not only the raw material—has a controlled human study.
- The formula version, market, and access date are recorded because ingredient lists change.

### Weaker traceability

- “Multi-peptide complex” with no INCI translation.
- A registered symbol with no identifiable owner or commercial grade.
- A high percentage with no statement of whether it means premix, solution, powder, or pure active.
- Borrowed supplier before/after images presented as if they tested the retail formula.
- A matching INCI used to imply authentic Matrixyl/Argireline without naming the supplier.

### Consumer decision rule

Pay extra for the supplier name only when it buys something you value: credible traceability, a disclosed commercial grade, formulation support, and evidence relevant to the exact active. **Trademark presence is not itself proof that the finished serum outperforms a good generic formula.** Conversely, the same generic INCI does not prove equivalence to the supplier grade used in the cited studies.

## 8. Practical takeaways

- **If you heard “one company makes Matrixyl,” that company is Sederma/Croda.**
- **If you heard “one company makes Argireline and related expression-line peptides,” that company is Lipotec/Lubrizol.**
- **If you heard about a huge patent library licensed through another ingredient company, Caregen and its BASF relationship may be the story.**
- A skincare brand may design the finished formula but buy the hero peptide technology from one of these upstream suppliers.
- A “multi-peptide” serum may combine ready-made technologies from several suppliers; The Ordinary publicly documents exactly that.
- Treat a supplier percentage as a premix/solution percentage until pure active content is explicitly disclosed.
- Supplier ownership and authenticity are separate from efficacy. The [ranked clinical evidence](02_ranked_clinical_evidence.md) still determines whether the peptide deserves attention.

## Evidence gaps

- Commercial supply contracts, raw-material prices, minimum order quantities, trademark-use rules, royalties, and exclusivity terms are usually private.
- Current technical data sheets with exact peptide assay are often gated to business customers.
- Product pages establish what brands claim to use, not independent batch authentication.
- Supplier product counts rely on commercial product-launch databases and should not be treated as audited market share.
- Generic materials with matching INCI names are difficult to compare without certificates of analysis, analytical methods, impurity profiles, stability data, and finished-formula testing.
- The Caregen asset count is current company self-report; the BASF agreement verifies the partnership model for four selected peptides, not use of every Caregen asset in cosmetics.
