IPL Dose, Skin, Hair & Course Simulator

Compare home IPL devices and professional benchmarks by wavelength cutoff, fluence, pulse splitting, skin tone, hair contrast, treatment area, coverage, and total course energy.

What this demo is for

IPL specs can look better than they are because "J/cm2" may be an accumulated burst, not a single pulse. This tool keeps the pieces separate: per sub-pulse intensity, effective stacked peak, accumulated dose per trigger, area coverage, and course total. The dot/window opacity in the map is driven by per sub-pulse fluence; the outline and course heat show accumulated dose.

Boundary: this is an educational model, not a treatment calculator. Home IPL devices in this topic are cleared for hair removal, not pigment, redness, rosacea, facial rejuvenation, or eye-adjacent use. Pigment/redness settings below are there to show why the home tools are usually the wrong lever, not to prescribe use.

Accumulated trigger fluence is the headline number. Per sub-pulse fluence is the opacity and peak-heating number. Gap determines whether sub-pulses can stack before the target cools.

How to read the depth view

IPL is broad-spectrum light, not a fractional microbeam. Lower cutoff filters keep shorter wavelengths that are better for pigment and hemoglobin near the surface. Longer wavelengths penetrate farther and are gentler on epidermal pigment, which is why many hair-removal filters sit at 560 nm or above. The home-device limitation is usually fluence and pulse structure, not just wavelength.

Why per-pulse opacity matters

Thermal damage depends on peak heating as well as total Joules. If 8.33 J/cm2 is split into 3 sub-pulses, the individual flashes are about 2.78 J/cm2. The map uses that lower per-pulse value for opacity, then uses outline/course heat to show the accumulated total.
