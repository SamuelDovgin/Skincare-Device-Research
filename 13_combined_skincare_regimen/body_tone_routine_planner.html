Routine for uneven dark body pigmentation

This planner is for acquired darker marks—PIH, friction, shaving/ingrown-hair marks, and rough follicular areas—not for bleaching a naturally deeper skin tone. The full evidence page explains the papers, concentrations, substitutions, non-topical options, and limitations.

Beyond topicals: the high-value ladder

For body skin, the intervention that prevents new inflammation can be more valuable than a stronger brightener. Use this sequence before buying a peel, laser package, or supplement.

What I would prioritize spending on
  1. Correct diagnosis and treatment of the active cause.
  2. Hair-removal redesign or clinician hair reduction when ingrowns are the repeating trigger.
  3. A calm, measurable 8–12-week topical plan.
  4. Only then, a focal clinician peel or device consultation.
What is poor value or high risk
  • Home IPL/laser marketed to lighten diffuse body skin
  • DIY high-strength glycolic, lactic, salicylic, TCA, or Jessner peels
  • Oral TXA without clinician screening
  • IV glutathione or unlabelled “whitening” injections/creams

Long-term maintenance lane

If your priority is months-to-years use with the lowest practical risk of paradoxical darkening, use a low-irritation maintenance design. No topical is risk-free and the supporting trials are finite, so reassess instead of escalating automatically.

AM
Gentle cleanse/rinse → choose vitamin C 5% or niacinamide 2–4% → moisturizer → tinted broad-spectrum SPF 30+ on exposed skin
PM 2×/wk
Cleanse → azelaic acid 10% to acquired darker area → moisturizer
Other PM
Cleanse/rinse → moisturizer only
After 3–4 calm wk
Increase azelaic acid to every other night only if needed and still comfortable; stay at twice weekly for folds/sensitive areas
Risk-prioritized exclusions
Do not make hydroquinone, retinoids, steroid combinations, high-strength peels, pigment lasers, scrubs, or unlabeled “bleaching” products part of an indefinite maintenance routine. If vitamin C or azelaic acid stings persistently, simplify to moisturizer, trigger control, and protection.

Kojic wash? Optional trial only: label-directed, rinse-off, no scrubbing, 2–3×/wk maximum to start. It is not equivalent to leave-on kojic acid and should not be used on the same night as azelaic acid initially.

Evidence is finite: 4% niacinamide was studied for 9 weeks, 20% azelaic acid for 24 weeks, and 5% vitamin C on neck/arm skin for 6 months. “Lower risk” is the design goal, not proof of unlimited safety. Azelaic-acid labeling still reports irritation and rare hypopigmentation, with specific monitoring advice for darker complexions.

Emerging melanogenesis targets

These are research candidates, not a shopping list. “Potent” in a human-tyrosinase assay does not establish body efficacy or indefinite safety.

Pick one path

Paths are ordered from most aggressive to least. “Aggressive” does not mean better; irritation can create more pigmentation. Start with the least intense path likely to fit your goal.

Most aggressiveClinician required

Path A — prescription spot protocol

Use only after a dermatologist confirms the pigment diagnosis and gives a duration. Most evidence is facial, so whole-limb, torso, groin, and large-surface use is not established.

Do not improvise triple therapy.
Hydroquinone, retinoids, and corticosteroid-containing formulas need a defined stop/review point. Do not use unapproved “bleaching” creams or apply strong actives over a full body area without medical supervision.

If azelaic acid repeatedly stings, replace it with TXA 3–5% or niacinamide 4%. Do not add all three to compensate.

Path D — barrier-first

Use for 2–4 weeks when the skin is irritated, eczema-prone, recently shaved/waxed, in a fold, or when the diagnosis is uncertain.

AM
Gentle cleanser/rinse → bland fragrance-free moisturizer → SPF on exposed skin
PM
Gentle cleanse → ceramide/glycerin/dimethicone/petrolatum moisturizer
Optional
Niacinamide 2–4% every other day if intact and calm
After 2–4 wk
Add azelaic acid 10% two nights weekly or TXA 3–5% daily—one change only
Fix the trigger first.
Reduce friction, stop scrubbing and fragranced products, and address acne, folliculitis, eczema, or ingrown hairs. A slower calm routine can outperform an irritating one.

Safety gates

Stop and simplify
  • Persistent burning, swelling, hives, blistering, open skin, or severe scaling
  • New light halos, obvious worsening darkening, or a spreading rash
  • Eye/mucosal exposure or pain
Do not self-treat
  • Pregnancy/trying to conceive with retinoid or hydroquinone plans
  • Thick/velvety neck, axilla, groin, or knuckle darkening
  • Unknown creams, mercury, unlabeled “whitening,” liquid bleach, or oral TXA

Key evidence anchors

  • 4% niacinamide for axillary hyperpigmentation — randomized, double-blind, body-site study.
  • 5% vitamin C on neck and arm skin — randomized 6-month study; useful long-term-tolerability anchor, but not a direct body-PIH trial.
  • 0.2% Thiamidol versus 4% hydroquinone — evaluator-blinded randomized facial melasma comparison.
  • PTPD-12 autophagy/melanosome-degradation peptide — 21-person randomized split-face study with 8-week facial evidence.
  • 2-MNG prevention study — randomized UV-induced pigmentation study in melanin-rich skin.
  • Kojic-acid contact allergy — delayed dermatitis can occur; a rinse-off wash is not automatically risk-free.
  • 20% azelaic acid in darker-skinned patients — randomized 24-week facial hyperpigmentation trial.
  • 20% azelaic acid versus 5% topical tranexamic acid — randomized acne-PIH comparison.
  • 2024 skin-of-color PIH systematic review — broad evidence map and applicability limits.
  • Long-pulsed 1064-nm Nd:YAG hair reduction in Fitzpatrick IV–VI — useful when ingrowns are the source of repeated PIH, not as a body-lightening treatment.
  • Nd:YAG hair reduction versus chemical peeling for pseudofolliculitis — small randomized study favoring laser for papule reduction, with procedure-specific limitations.
  • 2026 randomized low-fluence 1064-nm laser trial — no meaningful add-on benefit beyond the comparator melasma regimen.
  • Oral tranexamic acid trial — narrow facial-melasma evidence; not a generalized body-PIH recommendation.
  • Glutathione systematic review — IV use has inadequate efficacy/safety support and should be avoided.
  • FDA skin-product safety — mercury and unapproved OTC hydroquinone warnings.
