# One-Change Experiment and Tracking Guide

*Compiled 2026-08-23. Educational attribution aid, not medical advice, diagnosis, or a substitute for a clinician. This is a simplified personal log—not a randomized or blinded N-of-1 clinical trial.*

## Bottom line

When a routine has several credible products and devices, the highest-value next step is often **better observation, not another addition**. Freeze the rest of the routine, name one change, choose one outcome before starting, record a short baseline, and log adherence, irritation, and obvious confounders. The companion [regimen change tracker](regimen_change_tracker.html) turns that structure into a local, exportable record.

The tracker can improve attribution. It cannot prove causality, diagnose a condition, identify an allergen, determine whether a prescription is appropriate, or estimate a population treatment effect.

## 1. What counts as one change?

Use a change that can be stated in one sentence:

- “Add product X on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday mornings.”
- “Move adapalene from every night to three non-device nights per week.”
- “Remove fragranced cleanser while leaving all other steps unchanged.”

Do not bundle “new cleanser + new peptide + more red light” into one experiment. If the result changes, that bundle cannot tell you which component mattered. The American Academy of Dermatology similarly advises starting one anti-aging product at a time because simultaneous additions can increase irritation and obscure the cause.[[1]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/anti-aging/maximize-anti-aging-products)

Prescription or clinician-directed changes belong with the prescriber. Do not alter a prescription schedule just to make the tracker cleaner.

## 2. Pre-register the small decision

Before the first trial entry, write down:

| Field | Useful version | Weak version |
|---|---|---|
| One change | Exact product/device/schedule added, removed, or moved | “Improve routine” |
| Hypothesis | Expected direction and why | “See what happens” |
| Primary outcome | One repeatable 0–10 rating, count, or measurement | A different target every day |
| Direction | Higher is better or lower is better | Decided after seeing results |
| Worthwhile change | Smallest change that would matter to you | Any numerical movement |
| Trial window | A fixed review date appropriate to the intervention | Stop as soon as one good day appears |
| Safety gate | Product label/IFU plus the general stop rules below | Pushing through an unexpected reaction |

CENT 2015—the reporting extension for formal N-of-1 trials—emphasizes prospective planning, intervention details, outcomes, harms, and transparent participant flow.[[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26272791/) This consumer tracker borrows those documentation principles but does **not** implement the repeated randomized crossover periods, blinding, washout logic, or statistical analysis of a formal N-of-1 trial.

## 3. Establish a baseline

Log the chosen outcome under the unchanged routine on at least three representative observations before starting. Three is a usability floor in this tool, not a validated dermatology standard. More observations across the person's normal weekly variation are better.

Keep conditions as comparable as practical:

- same lighting, camera position, distance, and expression for optional photos;
- same body area and measurement method;
- similar time of day;
- the same outcome definition;
- notes for menstrual cycle, illness, unusual sun/heat exposure, travel, procedures, medication changes, or missed routine steps.

Do not upload photos to this tool. It stores text and numeric entries only in this browser's local storage.

## 4. Run and log the trial

The tracker records:

1. phase: baseline or trial;
2. primary outcome value;
3. irritation/discomfort on a separate 0–10 scale;
4. whether the planned change was followed;
5. whether an obvious confounder occurred; and
6. a short note.

The summary compares simple baseline and trial means after orienting the result so that a positive number always means movement in the pre-specified favorable direction. It labels the result “directional signal,” “no clear signal,” or “directional worsening” only after the user-entered worthwhile-change threshold is crossed. This is descriptive math, not inferential statistics.

## 5. Interpret conservatively

| Pattern | Reasonable interpretation | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome improves, adherence high, few confounders | A personally useful directional signal worth discussing or confirming | That the change caused the result or will work for others |
| Outcome flat | No clear signal in this window and measurement | That the intervention can never work |
| Outcome worsens | A directional warning; review tolerance, timing, and confounders | A diagnosis or specific causal ingredient |
| Irritation rises | Safety/tolerability may outweigh the target benefit | Whether the reaction is irritant, allergic, infectious, or another condition |
| Several changes or many confounders | Attribution is weak | That every change failed |

Avoid repeatedly changing the endpoint or threshold after seeing the data. Do not treat small day-to-day movement as proof. Consumer ratings can drift with lighting, expectations, regression to the mean, and natural fluctuation.

## 6. Safety boundary

- Follow the product label, prescription directions, or device IFU. This tracker never overrides them.
- For a rash, redness, burn, swelling, or another unexpected cosmetic reaction, FDA advises stopping the product and contacting a healthcare provider; serious problems can also be reported to FDA.[[3]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/resources-consumers-cosmetics/using-cosmetics-safely)
- AAD advises washing off and not reusing a product after a skin reaction; a severe reaction that is not relieved by simple supportive care may need dermatology assessment.[[4]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/prevent-skin-problems/test-skin-care-products)
- Urgent symptoms—such as trouble breathing, facial/throat swelling, rapidly spreading blistering, severe eye involvement, or signs of serious infection—need urgent medical evaluation, not continued tracking.
- Persistent, recurrent, painful, scarring, rapidly changing, or diagnostically unclear skin findings deserve clinician review.

## 7. What was added and why

The existing routine builder is optimized for **planning**: it exposes conflicts, overload, and coverage across a complex regimen. The missing piece was **learning after a plan is chosen**. This one-change guide and tracker add a prospective outcome, a baseline, adherence/confounder logging, a worthwhile-change threshold, and a safety gate without pretending to prescribe a regimen or validate efficacy.

## Evidence gaps and design limits

- No validated minimal clinically important difference exists for the tracker's generic user-defined 0–10 outcome. The threshold is personal and must be set before the trial.
- Three baseline and seven adherent trial observations are interface readiness rules, not clinical validation thresholds.
- The tool does not randomize, blind, create crossover periods, model autocorrelation, adjust for time trends, or distinguish placebo/expectation effects.
- Skin outcomes often require intervention-specific timelines; the tool deliberately does not invent a universal number of weeks.
- Local-storage data can be lost when browser storage is cleared. Export the record if it matters.
