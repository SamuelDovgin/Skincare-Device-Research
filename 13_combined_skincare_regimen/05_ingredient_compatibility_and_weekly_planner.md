# Ingredient Compatibility and Weekly Planner

*Compiled 2026-08-23. Evidence-led educational planning aid, not a diagnosis, prescription, pregnancy clearance, procedure schedule, or substitute for the exact product label, device instructions for use (IFU), pharmacist, prescriber, or treating clinician. The interaction review evaluates sequential use of finished products—not raw-ingredient mixing.*

## Bottom line

The archive now has a dedicated [Skincare Stack Lab](skincare_stack_lab.html): a seven-day AM/PM drag-and-drop planner, two-field visual pair explorer, and relationship map built from primary studies, regulator labels, and device instructions rather than a retailer or brand conflict chart.

The research does **not** support one giant “never combine” list. The useful split is:

1. **Documented direct-contact or duplication problem:** act on this first.
2. **Separate by session, label, recovery state, or clinician plan:** the ingredients may not “cancel,” but the use conditions matter.
3. **Additive irritation or redundancy:** simplify or alternate while establishing tolerance.
4. **Compatible in the defined context:** a study, approved co-formulation, or lack of a relevant mechanistic conflict supports coexistence—but not unlimited frequency.
5. **Unknown or formula-specific:** do not convert missing evidence into either danger or reassurance.

The small set of well-supported direct-contact problems includes conventional/unprotected tretinoin with benzoyl peroxide, dapsone with benzoyl peroxide, and hydroquinone with peroxides. Most other popular conflicts—retinoid plus exfoliant, retinoid plus vitamin C, or several acne actives together—are primarily tolerance and attribution problems, not proof that one ingredient neutralizes another.

## 1. Why this is a separate weekly module

The existing [single-day routine builder](routine_builder.html) answers “what fits my goals and one routine?” Its concern-coverage model, presets, and broad device catalog remain useful. It was not designed to represent alternating active nights, AM/PM separation, formulation-specific exceptions, or state-based procedure recovery.

The Stack Lab answers a different question: **“Where can each finished product or treatment live across one week, on one body site, and what evidence explains the relationship?”** Keeping both tools avoids flattening two distinct jobs into one crowded interface.

One plan represents one treatment area. Face, neck/chest, body/limbs, folds, and scalp are deliberately separate because vehicle, friction, occlusion, hair, diagnosis, device contact, and skin thickness can change tolerance. A green face-plan pair is not automatic permission for eyelids, mucosal skin, folds, scalp, or recently treated skin.

## 2. Verdict vocabulary

| Stack Lab signal | What it means | What it does not mean |
|---|---|---|
| Documented / duplicate | Label, direct stability evidence, same-use reaction, or duplicated treatment strategy | A forecast of severity for a particular person |
| Separate / label-check | Use another session, follow the exact IFU, wait for recovery, or let the clinician sequence it | That the products chemically “cancel” |
| Irritation / overload | Drying, sting, barrier injury, or attribution difficulty can rise | A proven molecular incompatibility |
| Compatible | Co-formulation or regimen evidence supports coexistence, or no relevant inactivation rule is established | Guaranteed benefit, zero irritation, or permission to premix |
| Unknown | Direct human layering evidence is absent or the result depends on the exact vehicle/formulation | Proof of danger; the safer interface action may still be separation |

The interface intentionally has no 0–100 safety score. Counts are detected rules, not risk probabilities. An internal ordinal “load” screen only identifies crowded same-session combinations; it has no physical unit, is not a dose calculation, and is never shown as a numerical harm estimate.

The pair explorer keeps all 38 catalog families visible in two compact icon fields. Pinning an item in field A recolors every candidate in field B; pinning B does the same to A. Hover and keyboard focus temporarily preview a candidate without changing the pinned pair. Green, amber, orange, red, purple, and gray are always paired with a symbol and text label for compatible, caution, separate/check, avoid/coordinate, duplicate, and unknown. Changing from same-session to AM/PM or different-day timing repaints the field only when the underlying rule is actually resolved; label-, recovery-, clinician-, week-, and evidence-unknown gates do not become green merely because the calendar changed.

## 3. Independent interaction map

### A. Direct or formulation-specific rules

| Pair | Best-supported classification | Planning action | Evidence boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adapalene + benzoyl peroxide | Compatible, with irritation monitoring | An approved fixed combination exists; sequential separate products still follow their labels | The 2024 AAD guideline strongly recommends benzoyl peroxide and topical retinoids and conditionally recommends combinations; the local FDA label documents the fixed pair.[[1]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38300170/)[[2]](source_docs/DailyMed_adapalene_benzoyl_peroxide_label_2026-08-23.pdf) |
| Conventional/unprotected tretinoin + benzoyl peroxide | Formulation-specific direct stability problem | Different sessions unless the exact tretinoin product or prescriber documents concurrent compatibility | A laboratory stability study found tretinoin degradation with benzoyl peroxide under tested light conditions; adapalene remained stable.[[3]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9990414/) |
| Microsphere/optimized tretinoin + benzoyl peroxide | Exact-formulation exception | Verify the exact formulation; do not generalize the exception to every cream or gel | An optimized tretinoin gel showed no degradation in the tested combination, and a microsphere label describes its protected delivery system.[[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20967192/)[[5]](source_docs/DailyMed_tretinoin_gel_label_2026-08-23.pdf) |
| Topical dapsone + benzoyl peroxide | Documented visible same-use reaction | Put them in different sessions if avoiding discoloration | The dapsone label reports temporary yellow/orange skin or facial-hair discoloration; this is not evidence that either drug becomes systemically dangerous.[[6]](source_docs/DailyMed_dapsone_gel_label_2026-08-23.pdf) |
| Hydroquinone + benzoyl/hydrogen peroxide | Documented oxidation and temporary coloration | Do not layer or premix; use different sessions/products as directed | An official hydroquinone monograph says simultaneous peroxide use can temporarily color skin because hydroquinone is oxidized.[[8]](https://pdf.hres.ca/dpd_pm/00052770.PDF) |
| Triple-combination cream + separate hydroquinone or retinoid | Duplicate prescription actives | Do not recreate or “boost” it with separate cards unless the prescriber explicitly directs this | The FDA-labeled product already contains hydroquinone, tretinoin, and a corticosteroid; it is a designed short-term prescription product, not a DIY template.[[9]](source_docs/DailyMed_TriLuma_label_2026-08-23.pdf) |
| Two retinoid strategies | Redundant/high-irritation strategy | Choose one; a prescriber controls transitions | Percentage alone does not make adapalene, tretinoin, tazarotene, retinal, and retinol equivalent. Alternating several makes benefit and intolerance harder to attribute. |

### B. Supported coexistence without a universal “cancel” rule

| Pair | Best-supported classification | Practical default | Evidence boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dapsone + adapalene | Compatible as a regimen | Follow prescriptions and monitor dryness | A 301-person randomized study evaluated dapsone plus adapalene and reported generally mild local reactions; this does not erase individual contraindications.[[7]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20120423/) |
| Niacinamide + vitamin C | No established routine-level cancellation | Same session is reasonable if the exact formulas tolerate and layer well; split for sting or pilling, not a mythical pH timer | A human hyperpigmentation study used niacinamide plus ascorbyl glucoside in one gel with ultrasound, showing co-formulation is possible; it was not a direct trial of separate low-pH L-ascorbic-acid and niacinamide serums.[[10]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16626384/) No clinical evidence located established sequential-use inactivation. |
| Retinol + vitamin C | A finished combination can work | Do not assume two independent maximum-strength products reproduce the study | Small double-blind human studies used a formulated retinol/vitamin C combination.[[11]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15767769/) |
| Tretinoin + topical ascorbic acid | Can coexist in a regimen | AM vitamin C / PM tretinoin remains a clear, tolerance-led default | A small clinical study included a tretinoin-plus-ascorbic-acid regimen; that refutes universal incompatibility but does not establish the best order for every product.[[12]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12633200/) |
| Retinol + glycolic acid | Co-formulation evidence, independent-layering caution | Use the designed combination or alternate separate leave-ons initially | A 40-person controlled study evaluated a finished retinol/lactose/glycolic cream; it does not validate arbitrary layering of separate high-strength acid and retinoid products.[[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18452434/) |
| Hydroquinone + tretinoin | Chemically possible in a designed prescription product | Clinician-led pigment course, not DIY mixing | Their coexistence in triple-combination cream supports formulation compatibility, while diagnosis, duration, corticosteroid exposure, and irritation stay clinician-controlled.[[9]](source_docs/DailyMed_TriLuma_label_2026-08-23.pdf) |

### C. Irritation, uncertainty, and order rules

| Pair or pattern | Stack Lab treatment | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Prescription retinoid + AHA/BHA/PHA/exfoliant | Different session initially; reduce frequency if reactive | Labels caution use with other drying/irritating topicals. This is additive irritation, not proof of cancellation.[[14]](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/downloadpdffile.cfm?setId=cabba2c1-cf07-46aa-a2c3-1b815517b16b) |
| Retinoid + azelaic acid or sulfur | Different session initially | These can share a broader acne/pigment regimen, but sting, scaling, and attribution load can accumulate. |
| Benzoyl peroxide + exfoliating acids, sulfur, or azelaic acid | Different session when tolerance is unknown | Acne combinations may be clinically reasonable; the planner separates the early trial because several drying/keratolytic products can crowd one session.[[1]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38300170/) |
| L-ascorbic acid + acids/azelaic acid | Tolerance caution, usually different session at first | Independent acidic leave-ons may sting together; there is no evidence-based universal “skin pH reset” waiting time.[[15]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29104718/) |
| L-ascorbic acid + benzoyl peroxide | Unknown/formulation-specific; do not premix | Oxidation is plausible, but direct human sequential-layering evidence is insufficient. Session separation avoids direct contact without pretending the clinical question is solved. |
| Copper peptide + low-pH LAA, acids, benzoyl peroxide, or HOCl | Unknown/formulation-specific | Copper/redox and peptide stability are legitimate formulation questions; a preformulation study is not a human layering trial. Do not premix; separate if formula certainty matters.[[16]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25384620/) |
| Several exfoliant families | Redundant irritation lane | Choose one initial exfoliant rather than stacking AHA + BHA + PHA + scrub. |
| Heavy petrolatum/ointment over a strong leave-on | Formula- and tolerance-dependent | Occlusion may support recovery but can also change penetration and sting. Follow the active label rather than assuming “seal it in” is always gentler. |
| Sunscreen followed by more skincare | Order warning | The primary sunscreen is the final morning skincare layer unless its label says otherwise; do not premix or mathematically add SPF values. |

## 4. Device and procedure rules are not ingredient “cancellation” rules

Device conflicts belong to five different control types:

1. **Exact label hard stop:** a named drug, implant, skin state, or contraindication in that exact manual.
2. **Exact-model setup:** clean/dry/bare skin, required coupling gel, eye protection, treatment site, or cadence.
3. **Recovery state:** hold high-load actives while the same site is red, swollen, broken, burning, or not back to baseline.
4. **Clinician sequence:** professional procedures and injectables require the treating clinician's pre/post care.
5. **Unknown/manual required:** the archive does not invent a class-wide number of stop days.

| Device/procedure lane | Planner rule | Evidence boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Red/near-infrared PBM | Use the exact manual; clean bare skin gives the most reproducible optical setup, then place topicals in another session | This is dose/setup control, not evidence that red light chemically neutralizes niacinamide, retinoids, or peptides. |
| Home fractional Tria FRX | Clean, dry, product-free treatment; bland recovery; active restart only after baseline comfort returns | The local IFU says not to treat persistently red/swollen skin. “Tomorrow” is not a recovery measurement, so the planner keeps the pair flagged across days.[[17]](../03_fractional_laser_resurfacing/tria_smoothbeauty/source_docs/tria-smoothbeauty-instructions-for-use.pdf) |
| Home IPL/diode hair removal | Exact model IFU controls photosensitizers, tan, broken/irritated skin, residue, and treatment site | A model-specific peel interval cannot be generalized to all IPL/diode devices or all topicals. |
| Home RF/non-fractional laser | Use the required gel or bare-skin setup and exact manual; avoid crowding the same reactive site with strong actives | “RF” and “laser” are not single protocols. Device, energy, contact, site, and recovery differ. |
| Professional microneedling/RF microneedling | Provider-only peri-procedure plan; do not drag an ordinary serum into the procedure | FDA says microneedling devices are not authorized to deliver cosmetics, topical medicines, or vitamins into skin, and has warned about serious adverse events with certain RF microneedling uses.[[18]](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/aesthetic-cosmetic-devices/microneedling-devices)[[19]](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/safety-communications/potential-risks-certain-uses-radiofrequency-rf-microneedling-fda-safety-communication) |
| Clinic laser/RF/HIFU | Clinician sequence | Parameters and wound response determine pre/post care; the consumer planner cannot supply a universal interval. |
| Sculptra/injectable | Injector-directed site care and procedure coordination | The patient label tells users to report recent laser treatment, mechanical/chemical peels, or hair removal because these may increase local redness, swelling, heat, or pain. This is procedure coordination, not a topical-cancellation problem.[[20]](../15_sculptra_plla_biostimulator/source_docs/FDA_P030050_S039_patient_labeling_2023.pdf) |

## 5. Catalog decisions: what was split, grouped, and excluded

The catalog was built from the topics already present in the archive, then narrowed to distinctions that change a routine decision.

| Archive material | Decision | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional tretinoin vs microsphere/optimized tretinoin | **Split** | Benzoyl-peroxide stability is formulation-specific and changes the rule. |
| Low-pH L-ascorbic acid vs vitamin C derivatives | **Split** | pH, stability, skin delivery, conversion, and human evidence differ. |
| Copper vs non-copper peptides | **Split** | Copper/redox chemistry creates a formulation question not justified for every peptide. |
| Hydroquinone, topical tranexamic acid, cysteamine, and prescription triple combination | **Split** | Prescription/diagnosis/duration and direct-interaction boundaries differ. |
| Adapalene, tretinoin, tazarotene, and cosmetic retinoids | **Split into treatment strategies** | One retinoid is not a percentage-based substitute for another; duplicate strategies are flagged. Retinol and retinal remain one cosmetic-retinoid lane because the current interaction evidence does not support more precise pair rules. |
| AHA, BHA, PHA, enzyme/scrub, and body urea | **Split** | Leave-on acid, keratolytic, rinse-off/mechanical, and thick-body-skin roles differ. |
| Benzoyl-peroxide wash vs leave-on | **Grouped as one active family** | Contact time matters, but the available direct-contact rules still require the exact label and vehicle. The card note tells the user to verify the formulation rather than inventing cleanser-specific clearance. |
| Kojic acid, alpha-arbutin, licorice, N-acetyl glucosamine, thiamidol, 4-hexylresorcinol, and similar pigment adjuncts | **Grouped as “other pigment serum”** | Human pairwise interaction data are too sparse for dozens of confident green/red cells; the exact full ingredient list is more important. |
| Home fractional laser, hair-removal light/laser, home RF/non-fractional energy, professional microneedling/RF-MN, clinic energy, and injectables | **Split** | Setup, tissue injury, recovery, professional boundary, and IFU control differ. |
| Face, neck/chest, body, folds, and scalp | **Split by plan context** | Cross-site extrapolation creates false reassurance. |
| Oral supplements | **Excluded and linked to the supplement checker** | Systemic dose duplication and medication interactions are not topical layering questions. |
| DIY raw powders, acids, preservative systems, and palm-mixing | **Excluded and linked to DIY formulation research** | pH, concentration, solvent, preservation, oxidation, and delivery become formulation problems. |

## 6. How to use the Stack Lab

1. Select one treatment area and set any relevant safety-context flags.
2. Start from a blank, foundation, or recovery week.
3. Drag or tap ingredient-family cards into AM/PM slots; place the exact product according to its label.
4. Resolve documented/duplicate findings before irritation/order findings.
5. In the pair laboratory, hover or focus a tile for a temporary preview; click or tap to pin it. Scan the opposite field by color, symbol, and accessible verdict text, then read the timing-dependent explanation and source in the result rail.
6. Treat “unknown” as a research boundary. Different-session placement can avoid direct layering, but it does not create evidence.
7. Sort generic layer order only as a starting point. Contact-time products, prescriptions, device gels, and exact labels override the generic order.
8. Save locally or copy the plan. Make a separate plan for another body site.

The safest default foundation is deliberately boring: moisturizer, one primary morning sunscreen for meaningful exposure, one retinoid strategy if appropriate, targeted treatments for a defined problem, and recovery space. More catalog coverage is not a goal.

## 7. Pairwise research update (2026-08-23)

The first Stack Lab pass had 160 same-session unknown cells among the ingredient-like cards. I searched the exact pair names with topical, formulation, randomized, stability, label, and sequential-use terms across PubMed, PMC, DailyMed, FDA material, ClinicalTrials.gov, and official product monographs. After a copper-peptide-specific re-audit, the expanded rule extract now gives a named category to **293 of 300 unordered pairs across 25 ingredient families**; **7 remain unknown**. This is coverage, not a safety score: a named category can mean an exact co-formulation, a tolerance/spacing default, a label gate, or a deliberate duplicate/avoid rule.

### Pairs intentionally still unknown

The remaining 7 cells are not an oversight. They are the cells for which the search found a mechanistic concern, an adjacent study, a marketing claim, or an unrelated route—but no defensible evidence for a stronger same-session category:

- **Niacinamide + HOCl; LAA + urea; LAA + benzoyl peroxide; vitamin C derivative + urea; urea + non-copper peptide; HOCl + TXA; HOCl + non-copper peptide.** The searches found no direct human sequential-use or validated finished-product pair study that would justify “compatible,” “separate,” or “avoid” for these exact catalog families. A BPO formulation study included vitamin C as an antioxidant, but it tested chemical stability in defined vehicles—not arbitrary skin layering—so the LAA/BPO cell stays gray.[[47]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34619676/)[[52]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18161634/)[[50]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6303114/)[[51]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12730738/)

The copper cells no longer display as gray because the evidence supports narrower categories. That does **not** mean every copper-peptide serum can be used with every card: the compatible cells mean “a named finished formula exists,” caution means “start in another session,” and separate means “protect against an oxidant/formulation boundary.” A current phase-2 registry still studies GHK-Cu gel against vehicle alone rather than testing combination partners, which is why direct arbitrary-layering evidence remains thin even after the taxonomy is more useful.[[60]](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07437586)

“Unknown” is a research result here, not a recommendation to use the pair. If a product label, pharmacist, prescriber, or full INCI/pH/vehicle review supplies better information, the rule can be narrowed without pretending that a family-level result applies to every product.

### Search method and limits

The search was run 2026-08-23 using exact pair names and combinations of `topical`, `formulation`, `stability`, `randomized`, `co-formulated`, `sequential`, `label`, and `clinical`. PubMed and PMC were searched first, followed by DailyMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA, Health Canada, and official product documents. Human trials and regulator labels were weighted above reviews; official INCI lists were used only for the narrower “exact formula check” category; mechanistic reviews were used only to support a conservative separation or to explain why an unknown stays unknown. Peels, injections, oral TXA, procedure aftercare, and a product containing several actives were not treated as proof that two arbitrary leave-on products can be layered. The archive also does not treat a lack of search hits as evidence of safety.

## 8. Design verification and falsification checks

The rule engine includes deterministic checks for the cases most likely to reveal a bad taxonomy:

- adapalene + benzoyl peroxide is compatible;
- conventional tretinoin + benzoyl peroxide separates;
- protected tretinoin does not inherit the conventional-formulation rule;
- dapsone + benzoyl peroxide separates, while dapsone + adapalene is compatible;
- niacinamide + LAA does not trigger a brand-chart cancellation myth;
- copper-peptide formula checks, caution splits, and oxidant separation remain visibly distinct rather than being collapsed into a universal “avoid” rule;
- two retinoid strategies remain a whole-week duplicate;
- Tria + a retinoid stays recovery-gated even on different calendar days;
- microneedling keeps routine products out of the procedure session while leaving separate bland care possible, and active restart remains clinician-controlled;
- every rule refers to a real catalog item and every citation chip resolves to a declared source.
- both pair fields contain the full catalog with exactly one pinned item per side, every tile has a valid verdict and accessible explanation, and the icon IDs are unique;
- the benzoyl-peroxide field differentiates adapalene, conventional tretinoin, and LAA, while AM/PM spacing repaints only the conventional-tretinoin rule as resolved;
- duplicate retinoid strategies use a distinct visual category instead of being conflated with a direct-contact problem.

The interface also tests native desktop drag/drop, touch-oriented placement, tap placement, mobile day tabs, local save, copyable export, responsive overflow, rendered-document routing, and source links. The self-test is available through `?selftest`; it tests implementation consistency, not medical truth.

## 9. Evidence gaps and conservative choices

- Pairwise human trials of separately layered finished products are rare. Co-formulation evidence proves that a designed vehicle can contain a pair; it does not prove that arbitrary products can be mixed in the palm.
- Direct clinical evidence for arbitrary low-pH LAA + niacinamide, LAA + benzoyl peroxide, copper-peptide layering across independent products, HOCl combinations, and many newer pigment ingredients is limited. The copper cells now use exact-formula, caution, or separate categories where adjacent evidence supports one; the seven residual cells remain gray rather than being force-labeled.
- No universal 5-, 10-, 20-, or 30-minute “pH reset” interval was established. The planner uses same session, AM/PM, different day, exact label/IFU, clinician sequence, or return-to-baseline skin state.
- The tool cannot inspect a user's full INCI list, concentrations, pH, vehicle, fragrance, allergens, prescription indication, device settings, or application amount. A future formula-level import could narrow rules, but only with auditable ingredient parsing and label metadata.
- Pregnancy/trying, persistent inflammation, severe acne, melasma, ochronosis concern, eczema/rosacea, recurrent folliculitis, infection, or professional-procedure timing can change the decision and deserve clinician/pharmacist review.
- “No detected rule” means the catalog has no applicable rule—not that a routine is effective or safe for every person.
