# Body hyperpigmentation: evidence-led tone-evening routine

*Compiled 2026-08-03. This page is research orientation, not a diagnosis or a prescription. It is for uneven body pigmentation that is darker than the person's own baseline—not for changing a naturally deeper skin tone. The most aggressive path is clinician-supervised. Evidence labels: **verified** = human or regulatory source directly supports the statement; **limited** = small, short, product-specific, or mostly facial evidence; **inference** = a conservative translation to body use rather than a directly studied body protocol.*

## 0. Bottom line

If “dark body skin” means flat brown marks left after acne, folliculitis, eczema, shaving, friction, bites, burns, or irritation, the highest-value sequence starts beyond topicals:

1. **Diagnose the pattern and treat the driver** if it is thick/velvety, spreading, symptomatic, or recurrent; do not assume every dark body area is PIH.
2. **Stop the trigger**: reduce friction, close shaving, fragranced deodorant, harsh scrubs, picking, and repeated irritation. If ingrowns are the engine, professionally managed hair reduction may be higher value than another brightener.
3. **Protect exposed areas** with broad-spectrum SPF 30+; for pigment-prone skin, a shade-matched tinted/iron-oxide sunscreen adds visible-light protection. Photoprotection is the foundation, not the final step. [[1]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39075672/)[[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39953770/)[[3]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/fade-dark-spots)
4. **Choose one pigment active and introduce it slowly**. More actives can create dermatitis, and dermatitis can create more post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH). [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20725554/)[[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37843491/)
5. **Use procedures only as selective escalation.** Clinician peels can be useful for small stable targets; pigment lasers are last-line and hair-removal lasers are for follicle-driven inflammation, not generalized lightening.
6. **Expect months, not days.** A few-shades-darker PIH spot often takes 6–12 months to fade after the trigger stops; active treatment may accelerate this, but recurrence is common if the trigger remains. [[3]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/fade-dark-spots)

The best default for an unknown body area is **Path C: niacinamide or tranexamic acid in the morning, azelaic acid at night, moisturizer, and protection**. If the priority is indefinite use with the lowest practical risk of paradoxical darkening, use the **long-term maintenance lane** below: vitamin C or niacinamide in the morning, cautious azelaic acid at night, barrier care, trigger control, and no hydroquinone by default. Use **Path D** if the area is sensitive, freshly irritated, in a fold, or prone to eczema. Use **Path A** only after clinician review.

## 1. First decide what is being treated

Do not use a “whitening” routine on every dark patch. The cause changes the safest treatment.

| Appearance / history | More likely lane | First move | Do not do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat brown marks after acne, ingrown hairs, shaving, bites, eczema, chafing, or a rash | Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) | Remove the inflammation/friction and use a slow topical path | Scrub, peel, or repeatedly irritate the area to “speed it up” |
| Rough bumps around follicles on upper arms/thighs with uneven color | Keratosis pilaris plus possible PIH | Treat texture gently; consider urea or lactic acid rather than treating pigment alone | Assume every rough brown area is melasma or apply hydroquinone over large areas |
| Thick, velvety, symmetric darkening of neck, armpits, groin, or knuckles | Possible acanthosis nigricans (AN) | Arrange medical assessment; ask about glucose/metabolic and medication factors | Treat it as simple dirt or keep escalating acids |
| Brown-gray patch with no obvious inflammation, recurrent central facial/upper-chest pattern, or light-triggered change | Melasma or another pigment disorder | Dermatology assessment and photoprotection | Translate a facial laser/peel study into a home body treatment |
| Itchy, scaly, ring-shaped, spreading, painful, oozing, sharply unilateral, changing, bleeding, or involving mucosa/palms/soles | Infection, inflammatory disease, or another diagnosis | Stop active experimentation and seek clinical evaluation | Apply depigmenters over an undiagnosed rash |
| Uniformly deeper natural tone with no distinct darker-than-baseline area | Normal pigmentation | No lightening treatment is medically indicated | Try to bleach the baseline skin tone |

### Acanthosis nigricans is a medical checkpoint

The American Academy of Dermatology describes AN as dark, often velvety thickened skin that may signal prediabetes, diabetes, thyroid disease, adrenal disease, medication effects, or another condition. Treating the cause can help the skin, while topical retinoids or other treatments are clinician-directed options—not a reason to keep adding acids at home. [[9]](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/acanthosis-nigricans-treatment)

If the darkening appeared quickly, is spreading, is thick/velvety, or occurs with increased thirst/urination, weight change, irregular periods, or a new medication, make the medical evaluation the first step.

## 2. What the research actually supports

The evidence base is not a single “body whitening” trial. It is a set of human studies across PIH, melasma, axillary hyperpigmentation, acne-induced PIH, photoaging, and rough follicular disease. The main translation limits are **face-to-body**, **small study size**, **short follow-up**, and **different causes of pigment**.

| Ingredient / intervention | Human evidence and studied concentration | Practical role in this page | Confidence for body-wide use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydroquinone** | 2% appears in modified Kligman PIH protocols; 4% is a common comparator in melasma studies and recent trials. [[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28114204/)[[10]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15008866/)[[11]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38707930/) | Strongest conventional depigmenter; prescription/clinician-supervised spot treatment only | **Limited for body**; most evidence is facial and short-term |
| **Tretinoin** | 0.025% has been studied in darker skin with acne and acne-related PIH, usually as part of a combination; 0.05% appears in clinician-led PIH protocols. [[7]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22798973/)[[10]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15008866/) | Turnover/retinoid branch when acne, follicular plugging, or PIH coexist | **Limited for body**; irritation can worsen PIH |
| **Azelaic acid** | 20% randomized, vehicle-controlled trial in phototypes IV–VI improved facial hyperpigmentation over 24 weeks; 20% was also directly compared with 5% topical TXA for acne PIH. [[8]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9829446/)[[12]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37213446/) | Best evidence-to-tolerability depigmenter for a non-hydroquinone path | **Moderate for pigment mechanism; limited for body** |
| **Tranexamic acid (topical)** | 5% solution performed similarly to 20% azelaic acid for acne PIH over 12 weeks, with fewer early side effects; newer melasma work also compares topical 5% with oral TXA. [[12]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37213446/)[[14]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40923777/) | Alternative pigment signal when azelaic acid stings; use topical only unless a clinician directs otherwise | **Moderate facial PIH signal; limited body evidence** |
| **Niacinamide** | 4% randomized double-blind axillary study in phototypes III–V produced significant colorimetric improvement over placebo at 9 weeks. [[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23355788/) | Low-irritation first-line option for axillae, friction zones, and maintenance | **Moderate for axillary hyperpigmentation; limited elsewhere** |
| **N-acetyl glucosamine (NAG)** | A randomized facial study used **2% NAG + 4% niacinamide** in morning and evening moisturizers for 10 weeks and found less detectable pigmentation than vehicle. [[31]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19845667/) | Lower-evidence cosmetic adjunct or niacinamide-formula swap | **Limited; facial combination formula, not body-site evidence** |
| **Cysteamine** | 5% overnight “sandwich” treatment has a 2025 randomized comparison with 4% hydroquinone in melasma; both improved outcomes without a significant between-group difference. [[15]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40127492/) | Optional clinician-guided alternative when hydroquinone is unsuitable; odor and irritation are practical limits | **Limited, facial and product-specific** |
| **Alpha-arbutin + kojic acid** | A 2025 split-face pilot used **alpha-arbutin 5% + kojic acid 2%** against a triple combination for melasma. This supports the combination as a research lead, not isolated-equivalence proof. [[16]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39555866/) | Lower-evidence cosmetic alternative for limited areas | **Limited; small pilot and facial** |
| **Adapalene** | A 2025 randomized trial of **0.1% adapalene** in female photoaging reported improvement in pigmentation and wrinkles, with early stinging/xerosis common. [[17]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40990960/) | Retinoid alternative when acne/follicular plugging is part of the problem and pregnancy is not a concern | **Limited for pigment; body translation is an inference** |
| **Lactic acid / salicylic acid** | A randomized keratosis-pilaris study used **10% lactic acid** or **5% salicylic acid** twice daily for 3 months; both improved lesions, with a larger mean reduction in the lactic-acid group. [[18]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25802513/) | Texture branch for rough follicular arms, thighs, elbows, or knees; not a proven whole-body pigment eraser | **Moderate for texture; limited for pigment** |
| **Glycolic acid peels** | Facial PIH studies used clinician-applied peels, including 35% glycolic versus 4% retinol peel and higher-strength protocols. [[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28114204/)[[19]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40970768/) | Professional option only; do not treat a 35–50% peel as a home body-lotion concentration | **Procedure-specific; not a home routine** |
| **Tinted/iron-oxide sunscreen** | PIH prevention reviews consistently identify sunscreen as the most reliable preventive measure; recent randomized and product studies examine visible-light-protective tint. [[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39953770/)[[3]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/fade-dark-spots)[[20]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41014037/)[[21]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41240206/) | Daily foundation for exposed hands, arms, chest, neck, and legs | **Strong for prevention; not a depigmenter** |

### Why the page does not rank “natural” ingredients above prescription treatment

Older reviews identify hydroquinone, azelaic acid, kojic acid, arbutin, licorice, retinoids, ascorbic acid, niacinamide, N-acetyl glucosamine, and soy as possible pigment-support categories, but the direct human evidence is uneven. [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20725554/)[[32]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29552273/) The best-defined lower-tier cosmetic adjunct here is **2% NAG + 4% niacinamide**, supported by a facial randomized trial; it is not evidence that a body lotion with an undisclosed amount of “glucosamine” will perform the same way. [[31]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19845667/) A small trial of a multi-ingredient serum is not proof that every bottle containing one of those INCI names will perform the same way. The percentage, vehicle, pH, stability, exposure time, and combination matter.

## 3. Melanin-pathway map and emerging candidates

“Melanin blocker” is shorthand, not a single biological action. A responsible candidate should be placed on the pathway it actually affects:

| Pathway / target | Established example | Newer or less-established candidate | What the mechanism can and cannot tell us |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tyrosinase enzyme**: lowers conversion of tyrosine/DOPA into melanin intermediates | Kojic acid, hydroquinone, azelaic acid, arbutin, vitamin C, resorcinols | **Thiamidol 0.2%**, **4-n-butylresorcinol 0.1–0.3%**, **hexylresorcinol 1%**, **KT-939 0.2%** | A strong enzyme assay does not prove penetration, body-site efficacy, or long-term pigment safety. Human tyrosinase selectivity is more useful than mushroom-tyrosinase potency. |
| **Melanosome transfer**: reduces delivery of pigment packages from melanocytes to keratinocytes | **Niacinamide 2–5%**; **NAG 2% + niacinamide 4%** | New delivery vehicles and peptide combinations | This can lower visible pigment without killing melanocytes, which is attractive for maintenance; irritation can still create new PIH. [[31]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19845667/)[[15]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12100180/)
| **Melanosome/autophagy clearance**: helps keratinocytes or melanocytes process pigment after transfer | Retinoids partly increase epidermal turnover but are irritating | **PTPD-12**, an autophagy-inducing palmitoyl dipeptide | This is a genuinely different target from tyrosinase. The first human trial is promising but only 21 facial subjects for 8 weeks. [[49]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41044809/)
| **Pigment precursor chemistry / immediate darkening** | Vitamin C and antioxidant systems | **2-mercaptonicotinoyl glycine (2-MNG) 0.5–1%** | 2-MNG reduced UV-induced immediate darkening and delayed tanning in melanin-rich skin; that is prevention of new UV pigment, not proof it removes old PIH. [[47]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38372022/)
| **Peroxidase / melanogenic signaling** | Not a routine cosmetic lane | **Methimazole 5%**; **glycinamide hydrochloride 10%** | Human facial trials exist, but methimazole is an off-label drug with endocrine relevance and neither has body or indefinite-use evidence. [[50]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31102345/)[[51]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32751779/)
| **Oxidative stress / glycation / inflammation** | Vitamin C, vitamin E, niacinamide, TXA, barrier and trigger control | **Ergothioneine + tocopheryl glucoside + decarboxy carnosine + naringin** formula; **Terminalia chebula 5%** | A multi-ingredient formula cannot prove which ingredient caused the change. These approaches may be useful when inflammation/oxidative stress is driving pigment, but the clinical data are short and facial. [[52]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40255950/)[[58]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41068344/)

### Candidates with the most useful human evidence

| Candidate | Human signal | Risk / translation grade | My conclusion for long-term body use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Thiamidol / isobutylamido-thiazolyl-resorcinol, 0.2%** | Randomized facial studies found similar or better short-term results than 4% hydroquinone; a 2026 vehicle-controlled study enrolled 200 participants for 12 weeks, while a separate 24-week trial included a regression phase. [[43]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33988887/)[[44]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41566113/)[[45]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34676600/) | **Best current emerging human evidence**, but mostly facial, product-specific, and one RCT reported allergic contact dermatitis in 2/25 participants in the Thiamidol arm. | The strongest “new ingredient to watch.” A reasonable limited-area cosmetic candidate if the exact product is authentic and tolerated; not yet a proven body-wide or indefinite routine. |
| **Hexylresorcinol / 4-HR, 1%** | A randomized 12-week split-body face/hand study found similar pigment improvement to 2% hydroquinone with no reported adverse effects; a separate 4-HR + niacinamide trial found greater improvement than niacinamide alone. [[46]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36502500/)[[53]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34958693/) | **Promising lower-intensity candidate**, but studies were short and largely skin types I–IV or Asian facial subjects. | A rational long-term watchlist option, especially if a well-formulated product is available; no direct body-PIH evidence. |
| **4-n-Butylresorcinol / rucinol, 0.1–0.3%** | Small randomized 0.1% studies and an 0.3% Indian observational study showed facial melasma improvement over 8 weeks with mild or no reported reactions. [[60]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20548876/)[[54]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26855596/) | **Promising but old/small evidence**, facial and short-term. | More credible than a generic “natural brightener,” but still not strong enough to outrank niacinamide/azelaic acid for body maintenance. |
| **PTPD-12** | 21-person, split-face, 8-week facial trial; melanin index fell and biopsies showed less epidermal melanin, with no significant adverse effects. The proposed mechanism is autophagy-driven melanosome handling rather than direct tyrosinase inhibition. [[49]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41044809/) | **Novel mechanism, limited clinical depth**; one small study, face-only, concentration/formula dependent. | Interesting research candidate if a transparent formula becomes available; not enough evidence to prescribe a body concentration. |
| **2-MNG, 0.5–1%** | Randomized intra-individual UV-exposure study in 33 melanin-rich subjects reduced immediate darkening and delayed tanning; 1% outperformed 0.5%, and both outperformed 2.5% 4-n-butylresorcinol in that prevention model. [[47]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38372022/) | **Good prevention signal, not a treatment-of-old-PIH signal**; all authors were from L’Oréal Research and Innovation. | Potentially valuable in a future visible-light/UV prevention product, but not a reason to buy an unverified raw ingredient or replace sunscreen. |
| **KT-939, 0.2%** | A 28-day study in women with sensitive skin reported good tolerability and improved facial spot/lightness measures; in vitro it was a potent human-tyrosinase inhibitor with reversible melanin suppression. [[48]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41159291/) | **Very new and very short**; one small cosmetic study cannot establish long-term or darker-skin safety. | One of the most interesting next-generation molecules, but research-watch only until independent, longer, diverse trials exist. |
| **Glycinamide hydrochloride, 10%** | A double-blind 8-week facial study in 21 people found reduced melanin index and no observed irritation; a 10% preparation passed a small irritation test. [[51]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32751779/) | **Small early human signal**; product-specific and not body tested. | Interesting peptide-like/α-MSH-related approach, but the high concentration and limited replication make it a watchlist ingredient, not a default. |

### Watchlist only: promising biology without a useful human routine yet

- **Hydrolyzed conchiolin protein (HCP):** a 2026 keratinocyte/cell-free study proposed lysosomal proteolysis plus hydroxyl-radical melanin breakdown, which is a genuinely new “clear the pigment after transfer” concept. There is no human clinical efficacy or long-term safety evidence yet. [[57]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41742231/)
- **4-hydroxyacetophenone (4-HAP):** preclinical mushroom-tyrosinase, melanoma-cell, zebrafish, and human skin-model results are interesting, but this is not a human body trial. [[55]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40821508/)
- **CHP-9 cyclopeptide, sennosides B/C, and kojic-acid-derived synthetic analogues:** enzyme/cell, zebrafish, or in-vitro studies only. Do not treat a docking score or zebrafish lightening result as consumer safety evidence. [[56]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40939092/)[[59]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38923657/)[[61]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39466938/)
- **Pediococcus acidilactici PMC48:** an 8-week, 22-person UV-tanning study reported improved melanin index with a strain-specific preparation; this is an early microbiome/formula signal, not proof that a generic probiotic skin product works. [[62]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37381171/)

### How this changes the long-term recommendation

For a long-term body routine, I would **not** chase the strongest enzyme inhibitor. I would select one mechanism at a time, prioritize non-irritation, and treat “new” as a reason to demand better evidence—not as permission to layer more products. The most rational research order is:

1. **Best current low-risk base:** trigger control, niacinamide or vitamin C, moisturizer, and visible-light/UV protection.
2. **Best established pigment add-on:** cautiously introduced azelaic acid, with monitoring for irritation or hypopigmentation.
3. **Best emerging human candidates:** Thiamidol, hexylresorcinol/4-HR, and 4-n-butylresorcinol—limited-area, product-specific, and not proven body-wide.
4. **Most novel mechanisms:** PTPD-12, 2-MNG, KT-939, and HCP—research-watch items with insufficient body and long-term evidence.
5. **Do not use:** raw laboratory compounds, compounded methimazole, unlabelled “peptide brighteners,” or formulas that hide multiple unquantified actives.

The realistic endpoint remains **less contrast from acquired darker areas without creating irritation, ochronosis, or new PIH**. A molecule that suppresses melanin strongly in a cell assay is not automatically a better long-term choice for healthy skin.

## 4. The four paths, from most aggressive to least

Choose one path for 8–12 weeks before judging it. These are **mutually substitutable paths**, not a shopping list to layer all at once.

### Path A — Most aggressive: clinician-supervised prescription spot protocol

**Best fit:** a dermatologist has confirmed epidermal PIH or another treatable pigment condition, the area is limited, and the clinician is willing to monitor tone, irritation, and duration.

**Why it ranks first:** hydroquinone and prescription retinoids have the strongest conventional depigmenting position, and modified triple-combination studies use hydroquinone 2–4% with tretinoin and a corticosteroid. [[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28114204/)[[10]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15008866/)[[11]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38707930/) But those protocols are mostly facial; applying them to a whole leg, buttock, groin, or torso is not an evidence-backed extrapolation.

**Clinician-set ingredients:**

- Hydroquinone **2–4%**, thinly to the darker patch only, for a defined course.
- Tretinoin **0.025–0.05%**, only if prescribed and appropriate for the diagnosis.
- A corticosteroid-containing triple combination only if the dermatologist specifically prescribes it. Do not improvise with hydrocortisone or desonide; corticosteroids can cause atrophy, stretch marks, infection, hypopigmentation, and other complications when misused, especially in folds. [[27]](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/psoriasis/treatment/medications/corticosteroids)

**Conservative order while waiting for the clinician’s exact directions:**

**Morning, every day:** gentle cleanse or rinse → bland moisturizer if dry → broad-spectrum SPF 30+ on exposed treated skin → clothing/shade when practical.

**Evening, alternating active nights:** cleanse → let the skin dry completely → apply the **single prescribed active for that night** to the patch → moisturizer. Do not add glycolic/lactic/salicylic acid, cysteamine, or an unprescribed retinoid to the same session.

**Example separation calendar, not a prescription:**

| Night | Active | Reason for separation |
|---|---|---|
| Monday / Thursday / Saturday | Hydroquinone 2–4% if prescribed | Limits continuous exposure and makes irritation easier to attribute |
| Tuesday / Friday | Tretinoin 0.025–0.05% if prescribed | Keeps retinoid irritation separate from hydroquinone and acids |
| Wednesday / Sunday | Bland moisturizer only | Recovery and barrier check |

**Duration gate:** review at about 8–12 weeks, or sooner if irritation or new light spots occur. Do not continue indefinitely because “nothing has happened yet.” FDA warns against unapproved OTC hydroquinone/mercury lighteners and reports rashes, swelling, and potentially permanent ochronosis with unsafe use. [[22]](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/skin-facts-what-you-need-know-about-skin-lightening-products/skin-product-safety)[[23]](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/health-fraud-scams/fda-warns-consumers-skin-products-containing-mercury-andor-hydroquinone)

**Pregnancy:** do not self-start hydroquinone or topical retinoids. Tretinoin labeling says not to use it during pregnancy; treatment decisions during pregnancy, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding belong with the prescribing clinician. [[24]](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=b5e385a2-f6f9-41cb-8a33-eedba397043f)[[26]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/anti-aging/retinoid-retinol)

### Path B — High-evidence non-hydroquinone rotation

**Best fit:** localized PIH, acne/ingrown-hair tendency, or a person who wants a stronger active plan without unsupervised hydroquinone.

**Core choices:**

- Morning: **niacinamide 4%** *or* **topical TXA 3–5%**.
- Evening: **azelaic acid 15–20%** if prescribed; otherwise a tolerated 10% cosmetic product is a lower-concentration option, not equivalent evidence.
- Optional retinoid: **adapalene 0.1%** two nights weekly if acne/follicular plugging is active and pregnancy is not possible; skip it if irritation is already present. [[17]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40990960/)

**Order:** cleanse → dry → one active → moisturizer. On exposed areas, sunscreen is the last morning step. Do not layer TXA, azelaic acid, and adapalene on the first night; start with one active, wait at least 10–14 days, then add another only if the skin is calm.

**Example week:**

| Day | Morning | Evening |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Niacinamide 4% → moisturizer → SPF if exposed | Azelaic acid 15–20% → moisturizer |
| Tuesday | Niacinamide 4% → moisturizer → SPF | Adapalene 0.1% → moisturizer, only if needed/tolerated |
| Wednesday | Niacinamide 4% → moisturizer → SPF | Moisturizer only |
| Thursday | Niacinamide 4% → moisturizer → SPF | Azelaic acid 15–20% → moisturizer |
| Friday | Niacinamide 4% → moisturizer → SPF | Moisturizer only |
| Saturday | Niacinamide 4% → moisturizer → SPF | Adapalene 0.1% or moisturizer only |
| Sunday | Moisturizer → SPF if exposed | Moisturizer only; inspect for irritation |

**Swap rule:** if azelaic acid burns/stings repeatedly, replace it with TXA 3–5% or niacinamide 4%; do not add both to compensate. If the main issue is ingrowns/acne, keep the retinoid branch and treat the cause rather than escalating pigment suppressors.

### Path C — Balanced, lower-irritation pigment path

**Best fit:** axillae, inner thighs, chest, back, or scattered PIH where the trigger is controlled but the person wants a sustainable routine.

**Morning:** gentle cleanse/rinse → **niacinamide 4%** or **TXA 3–5%** on the darker area → bland moisturizer → SPF 30+ on exposed skin.

**Evening, three nights weekly to start:** gentle cleanse → **azelaic acid 10%** cosmetic or 15–20% prescription → moisturizer.

**All other evenings:** cleanse/rinse → moisturizer only. After 3–4 calm weeks, increase azelaic acid to every other night if desired. Keep axilla and groin use on external skin only; avoid mucosa, broken skin, and freshly shaved or waxed skin until it is comfortable.

**Why it is a strong default:** the axillary 4% niacinamide trial is directly relevant to a body site, while azelaic acid has controlled darker-skin and acne-PIH evidence. The approach has a lower irritation ceiling than stacking hydroquinone, retinoids, peels, and scrubs. [[8]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9829446/)[[12]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37213446/)[[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23355788/)

### Path D — Least aggressive: barrier-first and trigger removal

**Best fit:** sensitive skin, eczema history, folds, active rash, frequent shaving/waxing, pregnancy/trying to conceive, or uncertainty about the diagnosis.

**For 2–4 weeks:**

- Wash with a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser; no scrub cloth, brush, exfoliating gloves, lemon, baking soda, or bleaching soap.
- Apply a bland moisturizer with ceramides, glycerin, petrolatum, dimethicone, or a similar barrier system after bathing.
- Consider **niacinamide 2–4%** every other day if the skin is intact and calm; stop if it stings.
- For friction folds, use clothing changes, breathable fabric, and a thin bland barrier layer rather than an acid.
- For exposed areas, use broad-spectrum SPF 30+; add tint/iron oxides where a suitable body or face product can be worn consistently.

When the skin has been calm for 2–4 weeks, add **one** of: azelaic acid 10% two nights weekly, niacinamide 4% daily, or topical TXA 3–5% daily. This path may be slower, but it removes the common mechanism that keeps making new dark marks: inflammation.

### Long-term maintenance lane — lowest-complication bias

Choose this lane when the goal is **a routine that can plausibly be continued for months or years**, with minimal risk of creating a darker or lighter patch. No topical has zero risk and the available trials are not indefinite: the body-site niacinamide trial lasted 9 weeks, the darker-skin azelaic-acid trial 24 weeks, and a 5% vitamin C trial on the neck and arms 6 months. This is therefore a **lower-risk maintenance design**, not a guarantee of unlimited safety. [[8]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9829446/)[[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23355788/)[[40]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12823436/)

**Morning, most days:**

1. Rinse or use a gentle fragrance-free cleanser.
2. Choose **one**: vitamin C **5%** (or a well-formulated derivative) **or** niacinamide **2–4%**. Start every other morning for 2 weeks; move to daily only if the skin remains calm. Vitamin C is a reasonable AM choice because it can support pigment/photodamage control and pairs naturally with sunscreen, but the body-PIH evidence is less direct than the niacinamide axillary study. [[3]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/fade-dark-spots)[[40]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12823436/)
3. Bland moisturizer if needed.
4. Broad-spectrum SPF 30+ on exposed areas; use a tinted/iron-oxide formula when visible-light exposure matters.

**Evening, two nights weekly to start:**

1. Cleanse and let the skin dry.
2. Apply azelaic acid **10%** to the acquired darker area; a clinician may choose **15–20%** when appropriate.
3. Moisturize.

Use moisturizer only on the other evenings. After 3–4 calm weeks, increase azelaic acid to every other night if the contrast remains bothersome. In folds or on sensitive skin, stay at two nights weekly or use niacinamide alone. Keep azelaic acid on external skin only and away from mucosa, freshly shaved skin, and open areas. The prescription 15% label still reports burning/stinging and isolated hypopigmentation, and specifically advises monitoring darker complexions for early pigment loss. [[25]](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=ab3f174c-a3c4-4ae9-b0bc-954efaadf8de)

**Long-term swaps:**

- If vitamin C stings or oxidizes quickly, swap to niacinamide **2–4%**; do not compensate by increasing acid strength.
- If azelaic acid repeatedly irritates, use niacinamide alone for maintenance or discuss topical TXA **3–5%** with a clinician. Topical TXA has promising short-term PIH data, but less long-term maintenance evidence than its marketing often implies. [[12]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37213446/)
- **Kojic-acid wash:** optional, lower-evidence adjunct—not an equivalent swap for a leave-on cream. There is no strong direct trial showing that a rinse-off kojic-acid body wash lightens body PIH, and the clinical kojic-acid literature is mostly leave-on facial products or combinations. If you trial one, follow the label, do not scrub, use it only on intact skin, and start no more than **2–3 times weekly** rather than daily. Do not use it on freshly shaved, eczematous, folded, or mucosal skin, and do not use it on the same night as azelaic acid or a retinoid at first. Itching, burning, tightness, rash, or new darker marks means stop; kojic acid can cause contact allergy, and irritation itself can create PIH. [[3]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/fade-dark-spots)[[41]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7720390/)[[42]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36566490/)
- Keep urea **10–20%** for rough elbows/knees as a texture moisturizer, not as a whole-body brightener.

**Deliberately excluded from this lane:** hydroquinone, tretinoin/adapalene, steroid combinations, high-strength peels, pigment lasers, scrubs, and “bleaching” products. That is a risk-prioritization choice, not a claim that these interventions never have a clinician-directed role.

### Optional texture branch — rough elbows, knees, or keratosis pilaris

This is not a stronger pigment path. It is for rough, plugged, or thickened skin that makes the area look darker.

- **Urea 10–20%** once daily is a bland keratolytic/humectant choice for thick areas.
- Or **lactic acid 10%** once daily or every other night; the direct randomized evidence is for keratosis pilaris, not body PIH. [[18]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25802513/)
- Or **salicylic acid 2–5%** for follicular plugging; the 5% trial was also in keratosis pilaris, so do not assume it is a pigment treatment. [[18]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25802513/)
- Do not combine the texture branch with Path A or a retinoid on the same night at first.

If the roughness is itchy, inflamed, widespread, or not responding, diagnose the underlying condition instead of increasing acid strength.

## 5. High-value options beyond topicals

A topical can fade some existing epidermal pigment, but it cannot stop a metabolic condition, folliculitis, ingrown hairs, close-shave trauma, or friction from creating new pigment. For body areas, the best non-topical intervention is often the one that removes the repeating trigger.

### 5.1 Highest value: diagnose and treat the driver

This is the highest-value branch when the darkening is **thick/velvety, rapidly progressive, symptomatic, widespread, or repeatedly reappearing**. A clinician can distinguish PIH from acanthosis nigricans, eczema, tinea, lichen planus pigmentosus, drug-related change, or another condition. Depending on the pattern, ask about glucose/metabolic testing, endocrine or medication review, and treatment of acne, folliculitis, eczema, or infection. Treating the cause may prevent new pigment; it is not a cosmetic “bleaching” step. [[9]](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/acanthosis-nigricans-treatment)[[30]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37843491/)

**Decision rule:** if the diagnosis is not reasonably clear, spend the next dollar on a dermatology assessment rather than on a stronger acid, home laser, or a large body product. If there are pustules, painful ingrowns, itch, scale, or an active rash, control that process before escalating pigment treatment.

### 5.2 High value when follicles are the trigger: change the hair-removal method

If dark marks follow shaving, waxing, or ingrown hairs, hair-removal strategy can have more durable value than adding another brightener.

1. **During active inflammation:** stop close shaving and waxing over the area. Use an electric trimmer with a guard, leave visible stubble rather than chasing a perfectly close shave, avoid picking, and wait until the skin is calm before any procedure.
2. **If coarse dark hairs keep causing folliculitis or pseudofolliculitis:** ask a dermatologist about a test spot and professionally performed **long-pulsed 1064-nm Nd:YAG laser hair reduction**. In a retrospective study of Fitzpatrick IV–VI patients, mean hair reduction was 54.3%; 86% had no complication, and transient hyperpigmentation was the most common complication among those who did. [[33]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21519944/)
3. **If the problem is recurrent pseudofolliculitis:** a small randomized study found long-pulsed Nd:YAG reduced papules more than chemical peeling at treatment end and 3-month follow-up, but the study was only 20 men and focused on beard/neck skin. This supports hair reduction as a trigger-control tool—not as proof that laser will erase old body PIH. [[34]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33559240/)
4. **After new lesions stop:** use Path C or D for residual marks. Hair reduction and pigment treatment are separate jobs; do not use a hair-removal laser as a generalized body-lightening device.

**Safety filter:** the operator should be experienced with the patient’s phototype and the exact body site, use a test spot, and explain the plan for burns, temporary hyperpigmentation, or hypopigmentation. Dark, coarse hair responds better than white, gray, blond, or very fine hair; if the target hair lacks pigment, laser efficacy is lower and another method may be discussed.

### 5.3 Selective clinician-applied peels: useful for small, stable, superficial targets

Office peels can be reasonable when the area is smooth, the diagnosis is established, the trigger has been controlled, and a dermatologist can treat a small surface rather than having a person repeatedly peel an entire limb. Evidence in darker skin includes clinician-applied glycolic-acid protocols for facial PIH and superficial peel studies; a newer skin-of-color scoping review still found small samples, short follow-up, and limited representation of Fitzpatrick IV–VI. [[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28114204/)[[19]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40970768/)[[35]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42306365/)

Published facial work has included **35% glycolic acid** versus a 4% retinol peel, but that is a clinician procedure with a defined pH, contact time, preparation, neutralization, and follow-up—not a home-body concentration. A PFB comparison also reported transient hyperpigmentation, erythema, and burning with chemical peeling. More intensity is not automatically more value in melanated skin. [[19]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40970768/)[[34]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33559240/)

**Use a peel only if all are true:** the skin is intact and calm; the lesion is limited; the clinician has a diagnosis and a test-spot plan; the patient can follow strict aftercare and sun/heat/friction avoidance; and there is a stop plan if pigment worsens. Do not DIY high-strength glycolic, lactic, salicylic, TCA, Jessner, or “body peel” kits on folds, groin, or large areas.

### 5.4 Energy-based pigment treatment: last-line, focal, and evidence-limited

Lasers can help selected recalcitrant pigment, but they are not the default high-value answer for diffuse body darkening. A skin-of-color review places lasers after topical therapy because response is variable and complications—including additional PIH—are meaningful. [[28]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36781686/)

The strongest reason to be conservative is that a recent blind randomized placebo-controlled trial of low-fluence 1064-nm Q-switched Nd:YAG for facial melasma found no meaningful advantage over the control regimen of mechanical exfoliation, hydroquinone 4%, and sunscreen; the patient-reported benefit also diminished by 6 months. Participants were Fitzpatrick I–IV, so this is not evidence for body skin or darker phototypes—it is a warning against assuming that a laser package is automatically additive. [[36]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40932145/)

**A reasonable clinic threshold:** consider a device consultation only after the diagnosis is confirmed, new inflammation is controlled, a well-tolerated topical/protection plan has had about 8–12 weeks, and the residual pigment is focal and genuinely burdensome. Ask the clinician what pigment depth and diagnosis the device is targeting, why that wavelength/pulse mode is appropriate, whether a test spot will be done, and how PIH will be monitored. Do not buy a home IPL/laser device for the purpose of lightening diffuse body skin.

### 5.5 Systemic treatment: high value only for a narrow diagnosis

**Oral tranexamic acid (TXA)** is a clinician-selected option for some cases of moderate-to-severe **facial melasma**, not a general treatment for body PIH, acanthosis nigricans, or natural skin tone. In one randomized placebo-controlled trial, 250 mg twice daily for 3 months improved melasma severity more than placebo, but the studied indication and route do not transfer to body marks. Oral TXA has systemic clotting-risk considerations and should not be self-started or swapped in for topical TXA. [[14]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40923777/)[[37]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28987494/)

**Do not pay for IV “skin-lightening” glutathione.** A systematic review found inadequate efficacy and safety evidence for IV use and concluded it is contraindicated; FDA has also reported serious adverse events from compounded injectable glutathione contaminated with excessive endotoxin. This is a poor value-to-risk tradeoff for cosmetic body lightening. [[38]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39444151/)[[39]](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/fda-highlights-concerns-using-dietary-ingredient-glutathione-compound-sterile-injectables)

### 5.6 The highest-value sequence in real life

| Stage | What to do | When to add the next stage |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Diagnose | Confirm PIH versus AN, follicular disease, eczema, infection, or another pigment disorder | Before strong actives or procedures if the pattern is atypical, thick/velvety, symptomatic, or spreading |
| 2. Stop new injury | Friction reduction, guarded trimming, no picking, bland barrier care, and treatment of active acne/folliculitis/rash | Give the site about 2–4 calm weeks; ongoing new lesions mean this stage is incomplete |
| 3. Treat residual pigment | Choose Path A–D; one pigment active at a time, with protection on exposed areas | Reassess around 8–12 weeks using standardized photographs |
| 4. Escalate selectively | For a limited stable target: clinician peel; for follicle-driven disease: hair-reduction consultation; for refractory focal pigment: device consultation | Only after diagnosis, trigger control, informed consent, and a test-spot/aftercare plan |
| 5. Maintain | Keep the trigger-control method, moisturizer, and protection; use the minimum active schedule that remains useful | Indefinitely, because recurrence is common when the driver returns |

## 6. Ingredient swap matrix: one role, one active at a time

“Swap” means choose one role-appropriate option when the original is unavailable, irritating, too expensive, or unsuitable. It does **not** mean use every item in a row together.

| Role | First choice | Science-backed swaps | Concentration / placement | Main caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pigment suppression, highest intensity | Hydroquinone | Azelaic acid; topical TXA; cysteamine | HQ 2–4% clinician-set; azelaic 15–20%; TXA 3–5%; cysteamine 5% | HQ/cysteamine are clinician-guided; body evidence is thin |
| Long-term maintenance bias | Niacinamide 2–4% **or** vitamin C 5% | Azelaic acid 10%; topical TXA 3–5% | AM: niacinamide or vitamin C; PM: azelaic acid 2×/week, then every other night if calm | Lower-risk design, not indefinite safety proof; stop for persistent irritation or pigment loss |
| Wash-off adjunct | Kojic-acid wash, label concentration | Gentle fragrance-free cleanser | Rinse-off; start 2–3×/week if trialled, never scrub | No strong direct body-wash evidence; contact allergy and irritation remain possible |
| Pigment support, lower irritation | Niacinamide 4% | TXA; azelaic acid 10% | Niacinamide 4%; TXA 3–5%; azelaic 10% | 4% niacinamide has body-site evidence; other swaps are mostly facial |
| Cosmetic adjunct | Niacinamide 4% | N-acetyl glucosamine 2% + niacinamide 4% | Use as a finished formula, not as a DIY powder mix | Randomized evidence is facial and combination-specific |
| Retinoid/turnover | Tretinoin | Adapalene | Tretinoin 0.025–0.05%; adapalene 0.1% | Not during pregnancy; irritation can worsen PIH |
| Cosmetic depigmenter alternative | Alpha-arbutin + kojic acid | Niacinamide; azelaic acid | Pilot used alpha-arbutin 5% + kojic acid 2% | Combination pilot is not proof of isolated ingredient equivalence |
| Roughness/follicular plugging | Lactic acid | Salicylic acid; urea | Lactic 10%; salicylic 2–5%; urea 10–20% | Treats texture, not necessarily melanogenesis |
| Light-trigger prevention | Tinted broad-spectrum sunscreen with iron oxides | Untinted broad-spectrum SPF 30+ plus clothing/shade | SPF 30+; water-resistant when sweating/swimming | Tint helps visible-light protection but is formula/shade dependent |
| Barrier support | Ceramide/glycerin/dimethicone/petrolatum moisturizer | Plain fragrance-free emollient | Use after bathing and after actives | Does not lighten; it lowers irritation and relapse risk |

### Options deliberately not used as DIY swaps

- **Mercury, calomel, mercurous chloride, mercuric, mercurio, or Hg:** avoid. Mercury is banned in skin-lightening products and can cause neurologic, kidney, skin, and other toxicity. [[22]](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/skin-facts-what-you-need-know-about-skin-lightening-products/skin-product-safety)[[23]](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/health-fraud-scams/fda-warns-consumers-skin-products-containing-mercury-andor-hydroquinone)
- **Unlabeled “bleaching,” “whitening,” or handmade creams:** avoid. The label may not disclose hydroquinone, mercury, potent steroids, or other drugs. [[22]](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/skin-facts-what-you-need-know-about-skin-lightening-products/skin-product-safety)[[23]](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/health-fraud-scams/fda-warns-consumers-skin-products-containing-mercury-andor-hydroquinone)
- **Liquid bleach:** never apply to skin. [[3]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/fade-dark-spots)
- **Topical corticosteroid as a brightener:** not a routine ingredient. A short trial of desonide in axillary hyperpigmentation does not make steroid self-treatment safe; the drug label includes atrophy, striae, folliculitis, infection, and hypopigmentation among possible adverse effects. [[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23355788/)[[27]](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/psoriasis/treatment/medications/corticosteroids)
- **Oral tranexamic acid:** not a topical swap. Oral TXA has systemic clotting-risk screening and should only be considered by a clinician. The 2025 oral-versus-topical trial does not erase that difference. [[14]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40923777/)
- **Home IPL, laser, or aggressive peels for body pigment:** do not infer pigment safety from hair-removal clearance or a clinic facial study. Lasers are second-line to topical agents for PIH in skin of color because response and complication risk are variable. [[28]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36781686/)

## 7. Product order and body-area rules

### Standard morning order

1. Gentle rinse or fragrance-free cleanser.
2. One water-based pigment active, if used: niacinamide **or** topical TXA.
3. Moisturizer if needed.
4. Broad-spectrum SPF 30+ on exposed skin; use tinted/iron-oxide protection when pigment and visible light are concerns.
5. Clothing/shade. Reapply about every 2 hours outdoors and after sweating/swimming according to the sunscreen label. [[3]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/fade-dark-spots)

### Standard evening order

1. Cleanse, rinse thoroughly, and pat dry—no scrubbing.
2. Wait until skin is fully dry, especially before a retinoid or acid.
3. Apply one selected active in a thin film to the darker area; do not coat uninvolved natural skin.
4. Moisturizer.

### Body-area adjustments

| Area | Prefer | Use caution with |
|---|---|---|
| Axillae | Niacinamide 4%, azelaic acid 10% slowly, fragrance-free deodorant, friction reduction | High-strength acids, retinoids, freshly shaved skin, steroid brightening |
| Groin / inner thigh | Barrier-first, niacinamide, clinician-guided azelaic acid on external skin | Mucosa, hydroquinone without supervision, acids after shaving, fragranced products |
| Elbows / knees | Urea 10–20% or lactic acid 10% for roughness, then pigment path if still needed | Treating thick scale with high-frequency peels or scrubbing |
| Upper arms / thighs with follicular bumps | Treat keratosis pilaris/ingrowns; lactic acid 10% or urea | Layering lactic + salicylic + retinoid immediately |
| Chest / back | Acne/folliculitis control, azelaic acid or retinoid if appropriate, SPF when exposed | Picking, occlusive fragranced oils, treating active rash as pigment |
| Hands / forearms / lower legs | Daily SPF and protective clothing; one pigment active | Expecting dark marks to fade while continuing sun exposure |
| Neck | Diagnose thick/velvety change before actives | Assuming all neck darkening is cosmetic PIH |

## 8. How to start without losing the evidence signal

Use a one-variable trial:

| Week | Action |
|---|---|
| 0 | Photograph the same 2–3 areas in the same light; record the trigger, shaving/deodorant routine, symptoms, and baseline color. Do not start if there is an active rash or infection. |
| 1 | Patch test the chosen product on a small area; wait 24–48 hours. If tolerated, use it on one small target area only. |
| 2 | Use the active on the planned schedule. Keep the rest of the routine bland. Do not add a second active yet. |
| 3–4 | If there is no persistent stinging, rash, scaling, or worsening pigment, increase only to the path’s planned frequency. |
| 6–8 | Compare photographs, not day-to-day mirror impressions. If there is no improvement or the diagnosis is uncertain, reassess rather than escalating. |
| 8–12 | Review response and tolerability. Clinician-supervised hydroquinone or prescription paths should have a defined stop/review point. |
| 12+ | Maintain trigger control, moisturizer, and protection. Continue only the minimum active schedule that remains useful. |

### Stop rules

Stop the new active and return to bland cleanser/moisturizer if there is persistent burning, swelling, hives, blistering, open skin, severe redness, marked scaling, a new light halo, obvious worsening darkening, eye exposure, or pain. Seek urgent care for facial swelling, trouble breathing, widespread blistering, or eye injury. AAD guidance specifically warns that burning or stinging means a product is irritating and that irritation can darken spots. [[3]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/fade-dark-spots)[[29]](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/melasma-self-care)

## 9. Evidence gaps and boundaries

- Most studies are facial; a full-body application changes the treated surface area, friction, sweat, occlusion, and exposure burden.
- The direct body evidence in this page is strongest for **4% niacinamide in axillary hyperpigmentation** and **10% lactic/5% salicylic acid for keratosis pilaris**, not for a generalized “body whitening” outcome. [[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23355788/)[[18]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25802513/)
- A formulation containing several brighteners cannot be reverse-engineered into proof for each isolated ingredient. [[16]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39555866/)
- A 2025 randomized topical-TXA study and a 2025 tinted-sunscreen study are useful but still product-, population-, and indication-specific. [[14]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40923777/)[[20]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41014037/)
- Darker skin types are disproportionately affected by PIH, but clinical representation and follow-up remain uneven; a recent systematic review found most cases were facial and that complete response was uncommon across treatments. [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20725554/)[[30]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37843491/)
- No routine can guarantee a particular shade change. The realistic endpoint is less contrast from acquired darker areas, fewer new marks, and healthier, non-irritated skin.

28. [Sowash M, Alster T. Review of laser treatments for PIH in skin of color. PubMed 36781686](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36781686/) — lasers are described as second-line to topical agents because of variable response and complication risk.
29. [American Academy of Dermatology: Melasma self-care](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/melasma-self-care) — visible-light protection, gentle fragrance-free care, and dermatologist evaluation for persistent pigment.
30. [Kashetsky N et al. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation: a systematic review. PubMed 37843491](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37843491/) — evidence synthesis and applicability limits for mostly acne-related, mostly facial PIH.
31. [Kimball AB et al. Niacinamide 4% plus N-acetyl glucosamine 2% for facial hyperpigmentation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19845667/) — randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled 10-week facial moisturizer study.
32. [Are Natural Ingredients Effective in the Management of Hyperpigmentation?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29552273/) — systematic review finding a small, heterogeneous clinical literature for natural ingredients such as soy, arbutin, licorice, mulberry, and ascorbic acid.
33. [Long-pulsed Nd:YAG laser-assisted hair removal in Fitzpatrick skin types IV–VI. PubMed 21519944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21519944/) — 150-person retrospective study; mean hair reduction 54.3%; transient hyperpigmentation was the most common complication.
34. [Evaluation of chemical peeling and long-pulse Nd:YAG for pseudofolliculitis barbae. PubMed 33559240](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33559240/) — randomized 20-person comparison; laser reduced papules more than peeling at follow-up, while peeling caused transient hyperpigmentation, erythema, and burning.
35. [Chemical Peels in Skin of Color: A Scoping Review. PubMed 42306365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42306365/) — 2026 scoping review; superficial peels may help, but evidence is limited by small samples, short follow-up, and pigmentary risk in Fitzpatrick IV–VI.
36. [Low-fluence QS Nd:YAG 1064-nm laser for melasma: randomized placebo-controlled trial. PubMed 40932145](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40932145/) — 2026 split-face trial; no significant added benefit beyond the control regimen and improvement diminished at 6 months.
37. [Randomized placebo-controlled study of oral tranexamic acid for moderate-to-severe melasma. PubMed 28987494](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28987494/) — 250 mg twice daily for 3 months in facial melasma; systemic route and indication do not transfer to generalized body PIH.
38. [Glutathione as a skin-lightening agent and in melasma: systematic review. PubMed 39444151](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39444151/) — evidence and safety were variable; the review concluded IV glutathione was contraindicated because of limited efficacy and adverse effects.

40. [Topical ascorbic acid on photoaged skin: randomized 6-month neck/arms study. 41. [Contact allergy to kojic acid in skin care products. PubMed 7720390](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7720390/) — case series identifying delayed facial dermatitis in patients using kojic-acid-containing products; selected dermatitis population, not a general incidence estimate.
42. [Efficacy and safety of topical agents in melasma: systematic review and meta-analysis. PubMed 36566490](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36566490/) — kojic acid showed efficacy in mostly facial studies, with lower pooled irritation than hydroquinone-containing combinations; does not establish rinse-off body-wash efficacy or indefinite safety.
43. [Thiamidol 0.2% versus hydroquinone 4% for facial melasma: randomized controlled trial. PubMed 33988887](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33988887/) — 50-person evaluator-blinded 90-day facial study; comparable improvement, mild adverse effects, and allergic contact dermatitis in 2 Thiamidol participants.
44. [Thiamidol 0.2% vehicle-controlled facial hyperpigmentation trial. PubMed 41566113](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41566113/) — 200-participant randomized 12-week study; significant mMASI reduction, no significant adverse-event difference, and Beiersdorf funding.
45. [Thiamidol 24-week melasma study with regression phase. PubMed 34676600](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34676600/) — 24-week vehicle-controlled facial study with post-treatment follow-up; product-specific and facial.
46. [Hexylresorcinol 1% versus hydroquinone 2% split-body trial. PubMed 36502500](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36502500/) — 32-person 12-week face/hand study; similar pigment improvement and no reported adverse effects, with limited skin-type representation.
47. [2-Mercaptonicotinoyl glycine prevents UV-induced darkening. PubMed 38372022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38372022/) — randomized study in 33 melanin-rich subjects using 0.5–1% 2-MNG; prevention model and L’Oréal Research and Innovation authorship.
48. [KT-939: next-generation human tyrosinase inhibitor. PubMed 41159291](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41159291/) — 0.2% lotion, 28-day sensitive-skin clinical study plus in-vitro and cellular evidence; too short for long-term conclusions.
49. [PTPD-12-containing cream for hyperpigmentation. PubMed 41044809](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41044809/) — 21-person randomized split-face 8-week facial trial; autophagy/melanosome-degradation mechanism and no significant adverse effects.
50. [Topical methimazole 5% versus hydroquinone 4% for melasma. PubMed 31102345](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31102345/) — randomized 8-week facial study; less effective than hydroquinone with relapse after discontinuation; off-label drug, not a self-directed cosmetic ingredient.
51. [Glycinamide hydrochloride human depigmentation trial. PubMed 32751779](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32751779/) — 21-person double-blind 8-week facial study using a 10% product; small early human signal.
52. [Terminalia chebula 5% versus hydroquinone 2% for melasma. PubMed 40255950](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40255950/) — randomized triple-blind 12-week facial study; comparable mMASI improvement and fewer reported irritation events, but extract standardization and body translation remain open.
53. [4-Hexylresorcinol plus niacinamide for even skin tone. PubMed 34958693](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34958693/) — 12-week randomized human study plus cellular/3D skin work; combination/formula evidence and not body PIH evidence.

55. [4-Hydroxyacetophenone as an antipigmentation reagent. PubMed 40821508](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40821508/) — mushroom-tyrosinase, cell, zebrafish, and human skin-model evidence; no human treatment trial.
