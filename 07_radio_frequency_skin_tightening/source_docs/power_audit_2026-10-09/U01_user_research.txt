5. What is MimiSilk Vera's likely real RF wattage?
A defensible measured estimate is not possible without an electrical load test. But the numbers permit a conditional estimate.

If the MimiSilk device is electrically equivalent to DLUS D2 and its 18 W rating is maximum input consumption, its sustained RF output must be below 18 W because the power-conversion electronics consume energy.

These are scenarios, not measured efficiency figures for MimiSilk. The device may draw substantially less than 18 W, regulate power during treatment, or use different electronics from DLUS. This table cannot establish even a reliable minimum RF output.

A wattmeter at the adapter could tell us real input power during operation. To establish RF output, a laboratory would need to measure the electrical power entering a characterized RF load and document the waveform, duty cycle, and impedance. Skin temperature measurements alone cannot substitute for that.

At $699, I would want these documents before treating Vera as superior to an established FDA-cleared device:

Exact OEM manufacturer and factory model number, plus whether the electronics are identical to DLUS D2.

Electrical adapter label and the distinction between 18 W input, peak RF output, and RMS treatment output.

Among these devices, I would still place more confidence in NEWA's FDA-reviewed 10 W load measurement than MimiSilk's unverified 18 W claim, and in Silk'n MultiPlatform's 20 W manufacturer-documented maximum than MimiSilk's marketing figure. It's probably a combination of thermal safety, electrode design, and the fact that home devices need to be usable without professional supervision.

5. My assessment for purchasing
Most interesting technically: MLAY RF01

Provisional
Of these three, MLAY has the clearest brand-specific specifications, with separate face and body power claims. I still wouldn't assume its 25 W face rating is actual RF power absorbed by skin.

The triangular machine may be stronger than a typical 10 W handheld, but the available OEM listings are too inconsistent to establish that. It has no verified skin-temperature cutoff, no actual load-test results, and no exact-model FDA clearance that I could locate.

The $39.95 ILIFT is the least documented. A low price alone isn't disqualifying, but we don't have enough electrical or thermal data to rank it against the others.

Would I buy the triangular $80.99 machine? Not yet, especially for use on the face or around the eyes. Its higher claimed output could mean faster heating, but without verified temperature feedback it also raises the possibility of burns or excessive localized heating. I would not use it as an experiment to see how hot it can get.

There is one thing that could make this investigation substantially more conclusive: a photograph of the power-adapter label and the rating/model sticker underneath that exact triangular machine. It could reveal whether we're dealing with the 24 V/3 A FREYARA-style design, the 55 W Allfond variant, or a lower-output unit.

That would help narrow down its actual electrical capabilities, although a calibrated RF load test would still be needed to establish treatment-output watts.
