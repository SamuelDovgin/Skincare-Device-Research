How to interpret the results
Three different forms of evidence matter:

Granted U.S. patent: The USPTO has issued legal protection for an invention. This establishes neither safety nor clinical effectiveness.

FDA 510(k) clearance: The FDA found a device substantially equivalent to a legally marketed predicate for specified indications. Clearance is not the same as FDA approval, and the cleared indications may be narrower than advertising claims.

Verified commercial product: There is evidence connecting the patented technology to an actual device, rather than simply showing that the manufacturer owns a related patent.

The strongest matches are those where the manufacturer explicitly identifies which patents cover a named product and the FDA database identifies that same product.

Important scope limitation: This is a targeted, source-verified investigation, not a certified exhaustive search of every patent issued in the United States. The technology spans many patent families and FDA device classifications. I distinguish confirmed patent-to-product matches from less certain associations rather than presenting every related invention as a validated match.

7. Limitations, safety, and how to expand the report
This search establishes a substantial set of actual FDA records and U.S. patent grants, but it does not prove that every listed patent is practiced by the listed FDA-cleared device. That distinction would require detailed claim mapping, manufacturer virtual patent markings, product manuals, and possibly technical drawings.

It also does not exhaust all devices introduced from 2016 to 2026. A full patent landscape would need systematic searches across USPTO/CPC classes, manufacturer subsidiaries, renamed products, patent continuations, and every applicable FDA product code. Lasers and light-based resurfacing systems have been excluded unless they also use a relevant RF or ultrasound modality.

There is a separate safety consideration: the FDA has raised concerns about complications from RF microneedling, so clearance and a granted patent should not be interpreted as a guarantee against burns, scarring, or tissue injury.

3. Does a higher temperature mean better collagen remodeling?
Not automatically. The relevant variables are the temperature reached in the dermis, how long that temperature is maintained, and how uniformly energy is distributed.

A 43°C surface cutoff could produce different results from a 40°C cutoff depending on electrode spacing, electrical impedance, treatment duration, and tissue depth.

Interestingly, CurrentBody's FDA documentation references tests on pig skin instrumented with multiple temperature probes. It reports comparable tissue-heating profiles to the TriPollar STOP U UXV predicate, but does not publish the individual depth-temperature measurements in the public summary. 

Access Data

For choosing a device, I would prioritize evidence of actual dermal heating and clinical wrinkle improvement over simply buying the product with the highest published temperature cutoff.

The biggest research gap: We have fairly good data for surface temperatures in these home devices, but limited public measurements of dermal temperatures at specific depths. Those measurements would be much more useful for judging how effectively each device stimulates collagen without unnecessarily heating subcutaneous fat.

Yes. NIRA is one of the more interesting alternatives to home RF because it uses a laser to create controlled heating within the skin rather than passing electrical current through tissue.

And there's another important candidate: the Tria Age-Defying Laser, which uses a different laser-delivery method and has published research showing actual microscopic treatment depths.

For collagen remodeling, I would separate technologies into two categories:

Thermal remodeling: RF, certain infrared lasers, and focused ultrasound heat tissue to trigger a repair response and collagen production.

Nonthermal collagen stimulation: Red and near-infrared LEDs can influence fibroblast activity without reaching the temperatures typically used for thermal remodeling.

Frequency alone does not determine depth. In RF, electrode geometry, impedance, delivered power, and contact time have major effects. With lasers, wavelength determines which tissue components absorb light, but delivered dose, spot size, pulse duration, and cooling also matter.

For example, 1,450 nm light is absorbed strongly by water, making it suitable for heating the upper dermis. A wavelength that penetrates farther is not automatically better for collagen, because it may deposit less heat in the intended layer.

The FDA's De Novo review records an acceptance range of 8–12 W, and the measured result was exactly 10 W.

However, the device uses pulsed operation: 300 milliseconds on per 750-millisecond cycle on the lower setting and 450 milliseconds on the higher setting. That corresponds to 40% and 60% duty cycles.

If the 10 W on-pulse value applies throughout those cycles, the approximate time-averaged outputs would be 4 W and 6 W, respectively, before considering changes caused by temperature regulation or skin impedance.

5. What I would actually rank highest
There are two different winners:

Highest publicly specified RF maximum: Silk'n FaceTite MultiPlatform, at 20 W. It has a manufacturer manual rather than just a sales-page claim, but independent output measurements and device-specific U.S. FDA clearance were not established.

Best evidence that a claimed wattage was actually produced: NEWA, at 10 W into 360 Ω in FDA-reviewed bench testing. Its temperature cutoff, pulse timing and clinical record are also unusually well documented.

For effective collagen remodeling, I would not automatically rank 20 W above 10 W. I'd want the energy delivered per pulse, duty cycle, load impedance, electrode spacing, controlled tissue temperature, and heating depth. A higher-output device can reach its temperature limit more quickly without maintaining higher temperatures or producing better clinical results.

One safety concern with chasing maximum wattage is unintended heating, including burns and potentially subcutaneous fat injury. More energy is only advantageous when its depth and temperature profile are controlled.

My next research priority would be Silk'n FaceTite MultiPlatform versus NEWA versus MimiSilk Vera: these represent 20 W specified, 10 W bench-measured, and 18 W advertised respectively. The crucial missing evidence is testing all three at the same electrical load and comparing temperature rise at several dermal depths—not comparing the headline wattages alone.
