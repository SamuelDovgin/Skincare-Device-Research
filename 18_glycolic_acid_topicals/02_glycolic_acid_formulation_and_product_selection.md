# Glycolic Acid Formulation & Product-Selection Guide

*Compiled 2026-08-12. This is practical cosmetic-formulation orientation, not a prescription or personalized treatment plan.*

## 0. Bottom line

For this archive's routine, I would look for:

> **5% glycolic acid, leave-on, pH disclosed around 3.5–4.0, in a fragrance-free or low-irritant emulsion/cream with humectants and soothing/barrier-supportive ingredients.**

If the face tolerates that for several weeks, a 7% product around pH 3.6 can be a reasonable step-up or body-area option. I would not start with a 10% facial formula while also using tazarotene and a fractional laser, and I would not buy a 25–70% “peel” for routine home use.

## 1. What to look for on the label

| Label/formulation feature | Preference | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Glycolic acid percentage** | 5% to start; 7–10% only after tolerance or for selected body texture | Fits the evidence and consumer-cosmetic framework better than peel strengths |
| **Final pH** | About 3.5–4.0, ideally disclosed by the brand | FDA/CIR guidance uses pH ≥3.5 for consumer AHA cosmetics; lower pH increases free-acid exposure and irritation potential |
| **Vehicle** | Cream, lotion, or emulsion for face/retinoid users; toner/serum can work if tolerated | A buffered emulsion can carry humectants/emollients and feel less sharp than a simple water-based acid |
| **Supportive ingredients** | Glycerin, propanediol, panthenol, allantoin, beta-glucan, ceramides, dimethicone, or a simple emollient system | Helps the product fit a barrier-maintenance routine; it does not cancel acid irritation |
| **Fragrance/botanical load** | Prefer fragrance-free and fewer volatile botanicals if reactive or pigment-prone | Reduces avoidable irritation variables; “natural” does not mean non-irritating |
| **Packaging** | Clean pump/airless or well-sealed bottle; follow the brand's storage instructions | Helps preserve the formula, but packaging cannot compensate for unknown pH or poor formulation |
| **Instructions** | Clear frequency, patch-test, sun-protection, and compromised-skin warnings | A good IFU/label is part of the safety evidence |

## 3. My choice for this specific routine

Given the existing Tria/laser and retinoid context:

### Face

- **First choice:** 5% glycolic leave-on emulsion/cream, pH around 3.5–4.0, fragrance-free if possible.
- **Trial frequency:** once weekly on a non-retinoid, non-Tria night; only increase if the skin remains calm for several weeks.
- **If that is tolerated:** consider 2 nights weekly or a 7% product, but do not increase both percentage and frequency at the same time.
- **If the goal is mainly pigment:** keep sunscreen and irritation control ahead of acid escalation. Glycolic acid is not a substitute for diagnosis, tinted photoprotection, or a prescription plan.

### Body

- A 7–10% lotion can be more reasonable for rough arms, thighs, elbows, knees, or other labeled body areas than for the face.
- Use a small area first. Do not apply to freshly shaved, waxed, sunburned, eczematous, or otherwise compromised skin.
- For keratosis-pilaris-type roughness, AAD lists glycolic/lactic acid, retinoids, and urea as keratolytic options and warns that overuse can produce raw, irritated skin. [[6]](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/a-z/keratosis-pilaris-self-care)

## 4. Scheduling with retinoids and devices

| Night | Default |
|---|---|
| **Tria night** | Clean, dry, product-free skin per the Tria IFU; no glycolic acid that session |
| **Retinoid night** | Use the prescribed retinoid as directed; do not add glycolic acid while testing tolerance |
| **Glycolic night** | Gentle cleanse → glycolic product → bland moisturizer if needed |
| **Recovery night** | Cleanse/rinse → moisturizer only; use this whenever the skin is tight, hot, flaky, or stingy |

The conservative spacing is intentional. FDA labeling for Differin/adapalene specifically says alpha-hydroxy/glycolic products may worsen irritation. Tazarotene is a different retinoid, so the exact interval is not transferable as a label instruction; the lower-conflict schedule is an inference based on shared irritation risk and the existing routine audit. [[7]](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2024/020380Orig1s021lbl.pdf)[[8]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/prevent-skin-problems/test-skin-care-products)

Do not use an acid to “push through” burning. A brief tingling sensation is not a required efficacy signal, and persistent burning, swelling, blistering, or darkening is a stop signal.

## 5. Patch test and stop rules

1. Test the exact product on a small area that will not be washed or rubbed away.
2. Follow the product's normal leave-on time and frequency.
3. Keep the test area under observation for several days; AAD notes that glycolic acid can irritate sensitive skin and gives a 7–10-day product-testing approach. [[8]](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/prevent-skin-problems/test-skin-care-products)
4. Stop and simplify the routine if redness, itch, scaling, worsening pigment, or stinging persists.
5. Seek clinician guidance for persistent dermatitis, eye exposure, suspected chemical burn, or a rapid pigment change.

## 6. What not to optimize

- Do not optimize for the lowest pH or highest percentage.
- Do not assume “free acid” marketing is a validated tissue dose.
- Do not stack glycolic acid with another exfoliating acid, scrub, peel, or retinoid on the same night just because the products are individually tolerable.
- Do not treat a 25% biopsy protocol as a home-product target.
- Do not use a toner’s low price or large bottle as evidence that it is safe for daily facial use.

## Evidence gaps

- Most retail labels do not provide the exact free-acid fraction or buffer capacity.
- pH can vary across regional formulas and reformulations; check the current package/product page.
- Product studies are not interchangeable: a 10% glycolic + phytic-acid emulsion is not evidence for a plain 10% toner.
- There is no validated “best glycolic formulation” for combining with this exact Tria + retinoid routine.
