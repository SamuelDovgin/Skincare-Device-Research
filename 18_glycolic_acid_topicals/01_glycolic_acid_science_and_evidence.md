# Glycolic Acid Science & Evidence

*Compiled 2026-08-12. This is research orientation, not medical advice. Evidence labels: **verified** = human clinical/regulatory source; **limited** = small, product-specific, or ex vivo evidence; **inference** = formulation or routine interpretation.*

## 0. Bottom line

Glycolic acid is more credible than a “glow” ingredient, but it is not a second Tria. The human evidence supports a smaller lane: **surface texture, discoloration/uneven tone, and possibly photoaging maintenance**, with a dose and irritation ceiling. The most useful study for a home routine is the 5% daily randomized trial; the most biologically dramatic study used 25% AHA on forearms for six months and should not be translated directly into facial home care. [[1]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9598014/)[[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8642081/)

## 1. What glycolic acid can plausibly do

Glycolic acid is a small alpha-hydroxy acid. In a leave-on formula, its effects depend on:

- total glycolic-acid concentration;
- the fraction present as free acid versus glycolate salt;
- final pH and buffering/neutralization;
- vehicle, water activity, humectants, and contact time;
- application frequency and whether the barrier is already irritated;
- skin site, phototype, and sun exposure.

FDA summarizes the mechanism simply: AHA exfoliation varies with the acid type, concentration, pH, and other ingredients. [[3]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/alpha-hydroxy-acids) “7% glycolic” without pH and vehicle information is therefore an incomplete dose description.

## 2. Human and translational evidence map

| Evidence | Design and formulation | Finding | What it supports | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Daily 5% glycolic acid** | Double-blind randomized placebo-controlled study; 75 volunteers; face/neck; 3 months; unneutralized 5% cream | Texture and discoloration improved significantly; wrinkle improvement favored glycolic acid but did not reach statistical significance | A realistic home-strength signal for texture/uneven tone | **Moderate** |
| **25% AHA biopsy study** | One forearm received 25% glycolic, lactic, or citric acid lotion and the other placebo; average 6 months | About 25% increase in skin thickness; increased collagen density and papillary-dermal changes; no inflammation evident | Human histology that AHAs can affect dermal/epidermal photoaging markers | **Moderate biologic evidence; poor direct home-dose translation** |
| **10% glycolic + 2% phytic acid + soothing complex** | 12-week facial emulsion evaluation in American women with Fitzpatrick II–VI; multi-ingredient product | PIH, hyperpigmentation, texture, and tone homogeneity improved from week 4 onward; tolerability was reported as good | A product-specific at-home cosmetic signal across a broader phototype range | **Limited; cannot isolate glycolic acid** |
| **8–25% glycolic at pH 4** | Human skin explants; daily gel/cream exposure for 5 days | Formulations stimulated desquamation/renewal and increased total collagen in the explant model without increasing TNF-α; authors proposed concentration tailoring | pH 4 is not biologically inert; partial neutralization can preserve activity | **Limited ex vivo evidence** |
| **2% and 10% glycolic emulsions** | Formulation and short in-vivo skin-pH/barrier study over 5 hours, 24 hours, and 7 days | Products acidified skin surface; the 10% sample had a stronger pH effect; no barrier impairment or irritation signs were reported in the study | A buffered emulsion can be acidic without automatically being barrier-destructive | **Limited formulation evidence** |

## 3. The “collagen” question

There are three different claims that are often collapsed into one:

1. **Exfoliation/texture:** the most direct cosmetic effect and the best fit for a 5–10% home product.
2. **Visible photoaging improvement:** supported by a small 5% randomized study, but the wrinkle endpoint was not a clear win.
3. **Dermal remodeling/collagen:** supported by the 25% forearm biopsy study and pH-4 explant work, but those protocols do not prove that a 5–7% facial toner will create a Tria-like remodeling response.

So glycolic acid belongs above “nothing” and below the archive’s stronger retinoid/fractional-laser evidence for collagen remodeling. It is a **moderate optional adjunct**, not a core energy-device replacement.

## 4. Concentration and pH are one package

The FDA consumer guidance page reports a Cosmetic Ingredient Review safety framework of **10% or less**, **final pH 3.5 or greater**, and directions for sun protection. That is a safety/consumer-cosmetic boundary, not a guarantee of efficacy. [[3]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/alpha-hydroxy-acids)

The practical read:

| Formula label | What you can infer | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|
| **5% glycolic, pH 3.5–4.0** | Sensible starter/face range; enough acid to be biologically active without chasing peel strength | Actual free-acid fraction, buffering, stability, and individual irritation |
| **7% glycolic, pH ~3.6** | Active leave-on toner range; The Ordinary publishes this type of specification | Water-based vehicle may feel sharper than a cream; botanical/fragrance-like components may not suit reactive skin |
| **10% glycolic, pH ≥3.5** | Upper end of the consumer-cosmetic framework; can fit experienced users/body areas | More irritation potential; still not equivalent to a professional peel |
| **25–70% or pH <3.5 peel products** | Procedure/peel territory, not a routine step | Depth, neutralization, exposure time, prep, and complication management require trained context |

The peel histology study found more tissue damage with higher free-acid exposure and more necrosis in very low-pH conditions. It involved only two patients, but it reinforces the basic boundary: “stronger” is not a safe shortcut to better long-term remodeling. [[7]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8634810/)

## 5. Pigment and skin-of-color relevance

The 10% glycolic multi-ingredient facial study included Fitzpatrick II–VI participants and reported improvement in PIH/hyperpigmentation. That is useful inclusion, but it is not proof that any 10% glycolic product is equally effective or safe across phototypes. [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33458927/)

For pigment-prone skin, irritation is the key failure mode: an acid that causes dermatitis can create new post-inflammatory pigment. Glycolic acid should therefore be treated as a controlled trial with a low starting frequency, not as a daily “brightening” obligation.

## Evidence gaps

- Few independent, long-duration, face-specific RCTs compare 5%, 7%, and 10% glycolic acid at matched pH and vehicle.
- Product studies often combine glycolic acid with phytic acid, soothing complexes, peptides, or other ingredients.
- Finished-product free-acid concentration and pH are often undisclosed.
- Histology does not tell us whether a cosmetic formula improves laxity, deep wrinkles, or Tria-like collagen endpoints.
- There is little direct evidence for combining glycolic acid with a specific tazarotene + fractional-laser schedule.

2. [Effects of alpha-hydroxy acids on photoaged skin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8642081/) — 25% AHA forearm application with clinical, histologic, and ultrastructural outcomes over about 6 months.

4. [Effect of glycolic acid, phytic acid, soothing complex containing emulsion on hyperpigmentation and skin luminosity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33458927/) — 12-week, multi-ingredient facial emulsion study in Fitzpatrick II–VI volunteers.
5. [Glycolic acid adjusted to pH 4 stimulates collagen production and epidermal renewal in human skin explants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32583600/) — 8–25% pH-4 ex vivo formulation study; not a human clinical outcome trial.
6. [Formulation of topical acidic products and acidification of the skin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33864274/) — 2% and 10% glycolic-acid emulsions with short- and 7-day in-vivo skin-pH/barrier observations.
7. [Histological comparison of 50% and 70% glycolic acid peels at different pHs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8634810/) — small peel histology study supporting caution with high free-acid/very-low-pH exposure.
