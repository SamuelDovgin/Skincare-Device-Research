# What the evidence actually supports

A useful purchasing trial would randomize the **same finished skincare formulation**, used with the exact consumer device or without it, and measure pigment, acne, wrinkles or tolerability over months. Ingredient delivery into the outer barrier is a surrogate. The studies below do not provide that missing comparison for your routine.

| Source | Design and finding | Purchasing limitation |
|---|---|---|
| [Huh 2003, vitamin C iontophoresis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12771472/) | Randomized double-blind split-face study, 29 women with melasma, 12 weeks; vitamin C versus water, both delivered by iontophoresis. Colorimetry improved on the vitamin-C side. | Supports the combined treatment; lacks vitamin C applied ordinarily as a control. Cannot isolate the device increment. |
| [Guo 2024, TXA iontophoresis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39185080/) | 30 participants, active versus placebo with iontophoresis, twice weekly for three months; melasma severity/luminance outcomes reported. | Also lacks matched TXA without the device; not a consumer-brand comparison or evidence for your Thiamidol. |
| [Taylor 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23377327/) | 35 patients, uncontrolled vitamin C mask/iontophoresis regimen with acids and sun protection; pigment improvement reported. | Multiple interventions and no control; the headline improvement cannot be assigned to current or vitamin C alone. |
| [Sobhi 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22360337/) | 14 patients, split-face vitamin C iontophoresis versus glycolic peel over six sessions. | A different treatment comparator, not vitamin C alone; very small study. |
| [Niacinamide 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12100180/) and [2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16029679/) | Controlled topical niacinamide studies found pigment/skin-quality benefits without a penetration device. | Supports ordinary topical use; does not exclude an additional effect from devices, but establishes a reasonable baseline. |
| [Thiamidol 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41566113/) | Vehicle-controlled study, 200 participants; 0.2% Thiamidol, 12-week mMASI reduction reported as 36.1% versus 16.1%. | Formula-specific benefit without a booster; no evidence here for electrically enhancing that product. |

## Medicube follow-up: which molecules were actually tested?

**October 1 correction:** the earlier review missed a published Booster Pro comparison and a Mini Plus assay excerpt. Both improve the evidence trail, without establishing efficacy for your vitamin C serum or a cross-brand winner.

### Mini Plus: an identified small-molecule tracer

Medicube's published statement identifies **Mini Booster Pro Plus**, Tego Science and testing dates May 8–June 2, 2025. A disclosed report page (NCR-25501-PR065, page 8/9) measures **caffeine elution**, comparing a control with device treatment. At the 15-minute sampling point, mean concentrations are **16.6 versus 103.7 µg/mL**, giving **6.25×**. At four hours the ratio is **3.17×**. The changing ratio matters: “625%” is a time-specific ratio, equivalent to about **525% more** than control, not 625% more. Fifteen minutes is the table's sampling time; the excerpt alone does not establish fifteen minutes of device use.

[Manufacturer statement capture](source_docs/medicube_report_excerpt_2.png) · [Manufacturer report-page capture](source_docs/medicube_report_excerpt_1.png), reproduced by [Seoul Economic Daily](https://en.sedaily.com/society/2026/07/07/apr-rebuts-korean-war-marketing-suspicion-over-medicubes).

Caffeine is approximately **194 Da**, ascorbic acid **176 Da**, and niacinamide **122 Da**: these are small molecules, not high-molecular-weight HA. Size similarity makes caffeine a relevant model compound, but **does not demonstrate the same transport gain for vitamin C**. Charge, pH, vehicle, oxidation and the sampled compartment still matter. [NIST caffeine](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?Name=caffeine), [FDA ascorbic acid](https://precision.fda.gov/ginas/app/ui/substances/PQ6CK8PD0R), [NIST niacinamide](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C98920&Mask=8EF&Units=CAL).

The excerpt lacks the complete protocol, skin/model identity, replicate count, device settings, dosing, sampling compartment and uncertainty needed for a full audit. It is not demonstrated in-vivo human delivery merely because the testing company also conducts human trials. This evidence belongs to **Mini Plus**, not automatically Pro, X2 or original Mini. No adequately documented Medicube high-MW ingredient assay or direct vitamin-C device-versus-hand test was located in this follow-up.

### Booster Pro: a small clinical comparison with glutathione

[Hur et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.52660/JKSC.2023.29.6.1496) names Booster Pro. Its EP arm involved 21 women, four weeks, a 500-ppm glutathione ampoule on both sides, and device treatment only on the left. Pigmentation and brightness favored that side. This is a matched-product clinical comparison, but manufacturer-authored, fixed-side, without reported blinding, short, and analyzed using unpaired between-side tests despite its paired design. It measures skin appearance, not vitamin-C uptake. [Public paper copy](source_docs/medicube_glutathione_2023.pdf).

The study's RM description also differs from ordinary RF-tightening marketing; it should not be used to recategorize Pro as a conventional heating RF device. Its device settings and formula are study-specific, not instructions to increase your active exposure.

### Lab Muffin: weighed in on Panasonic ion delivery

Michelle Wong's [December 2018 Panasonic article](https://labmuffin.com/ion-boosted-skincare-panasonic-skincare-tools-video/) discusses **EH-ST63**, including electrorepulsion of ascorbate and electroosmotic hydration. She notes that the clinical studies she reviewed lacked the same treatment without iontophoresis and did not use that exact consumer device. **The article/video was sponsored by Panasonic.** It is an explanation and review, not independent clinical validation of ST99/ST0A/SS85.

No directly attributable Medicube-specific verdict from her was located in the searched blog/YouTube/social-index results. Third-party assertions about her views were excluded. Her 2018 evidence-gap statement describes her review then; the later glutathione study above is a relevant exception, with substantial limitations.

**Implication for the rankings:** Pro now earns **C+ (preliminary clinical signal)** and Mini Plus **C (partial tracer evidence)** in this editorial rubric. Neither reaches B for your five-active routine. The earlier forced preference order should not imply Panasonic or YA-MAN is demonstrated more effective than Medicube. Source IDs S40–S44.

## Corrections to the pasted comparison

- ST0A’s 1.6× BOOST output is a manufacturer engineering claim relative to its predecessor. It is not a measured 1.6× improvement in your clinical results.
- ST99’s earlier pasted peptide 6.3× figure was not reconfirmed in a primary source. The official regional catalog supports HA 5.7× and collagen 5.0×; keep the ingredient identity clear. [Panasonic catalog](https://pstw.panasonic.com.tw/catalog/files/1_consumer/hair/202509_Panasonic_%E7%BE%8E%E5%AE%B9%E5%95%86%E5%93%81.pdf).
- YA-MAN “more warmth” or “stronger EMS sensation” comparisons are not RF wattage or penetration measurements.
- Older Medicube “785%” marketing should not be ranked against Panasonic’s HA multiple without the underlying assay and denominator. It was not reconfirmed on the current official Pro page used here.
- High-output delivery tests do not prove dermal collagen stimulation; additional RF or EMS features require their own evidence.

**What this section found:** small clinical studies support certain active-plus-ion regimens; no located trial establishes the incremental value or best consumer model for the user’s complete routine. Industry tests provide narrower delivery endpoints. S04–S15, S21–S24.
