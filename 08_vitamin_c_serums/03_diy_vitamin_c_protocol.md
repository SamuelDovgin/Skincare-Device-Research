# DIY Vitamin C Protocol: Fast, Fresh, and Less Annoying

*Compiled 2026-07-04. This is practical cosmetic chemistry orientation, not medical advice.*

## 0. Bottom line

The simplest DIY serum that makes sense is:

> **L-ascorbic acid powder + distilled water + careful pH adjustment + tiny weekly batch + fridge + light protection.**

Do not try to make a shelf-stable SkinCeuticals clone in a kitchen. The realistic DIY advantage is freshness and cost, not long-term stability.

## 1. When to use with Differin

Use:

- **Morning:** vitamin C serum -> moisturizer if needed -> sunscreen.
- **Night:** Differin/adapalene -> moisturizer.

Why separate them:

- low-pH L-ascorbic acid can sting;
- Differin can dry/irritate;
- irritation can worsen post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in pigment-prone skin;
- vitamin C is most useful as daytime antioxidant support under sunscreen.

If your skin is annoyed: use vitamin C every other morning or 3 mornings/week.

## 2. What concentration to choose

| Concentration | Best for | Read |
|---|---|---|
| **10%** | First DIY batch, sensitive skin, Differin users | Best starter |
| **15%** | Most CEF-like strength without going all-in | Good default |
| **20%** | Maximum classic absorption range | More sting; not automatically better |

If skipping pH strips, start at **10-15%**, not 20%.

## 3. Exact quick recipes for a 36.67 mL batch

These match the previous working target of **2/3 of 55 mL = 36.67 mL final water amount** and target roughly **pH 3.30** using partial neutralization with baking soda.

Assumption:

- pKa L-ascorbic acid ~= 4.10
- target pH ~= 3.30
- fraction neutralized ~= 13.67%
- **baking soda grams = L-ascorbic acid grams x 0.0652**

| Final strength | L-ascorbic acid | Baking soda | Distilled water |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **10%** | 3.67 g | 0.24 g | 36.67 g total |
| **15%** | 5.50 g | 0.36 g | 36.67 g total |
| **20%** | 7.33 g | 0.48 g | 36.67 g total |

Important: this is a calculated estimate, not a measured pH guarantee. Powders, water, scale accuracy, CO2, and volume marks can shift the real value.

## 4. Fast mixing steps

Best fast method:

1. Add L-ascorbic acid powder to the clean bottle.
2. Add about 80-90% of the distilled water.
3. Cap and shake until dissolved.
4. Add baking soda.
5. Cap loosely and swirl. It will fizz.
6. Do **not** fully tighten while actively fizzing.
7. When fizzing calms, tighten and gently shake.
8. Top up with the remaining distilled water.
9. Let it sit 20-30 minutes to degas.
10. Wrap bottle in foil or use an opaque/amber bottle.
11. Refrigerate.

## 5. Can you add all powders first?

Yes, but it is messier.

What can go wrong:

- baking soda + acid makes CO2 gas;
- if capped, pressure can build;
- powder clumps can dissolve unevenly;
- dissolved CO2 can make the mix feel/read temporarily more acidic;
- foaming can overflow and change your final concentration.

If you insist:

1. Add both powders to an open bottle.
2. Add water slowly in 2-3 pours.
3. Swirl between pours.
4. Keep the cap loose until fizzing stops.
5. Shake only after the active fizz has calmed.

## 6. pH strips: what to buy

Best:

- narrow-range strips around **pH 2.8-4.4** or **pH 0-6**.

Okay:

- universal strips if they distinguish the 3-4 range clearly.

Annoying:

- strips where adjacent colors are too similar in the 3-4 range.

You do not need a "green" color. You are not aiming for neutral pH. A useful vitamin C serum is acidic; for L-ascorbic acid, the target is commonly around **pH 3-3.5**.

## 7. No-strip risk management

If you are skipping strips:

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Use exact gram weights | Volume spoon measurements drift |
| Start at 10-15% | Less sting risk |
| Use distilled water | Metal ions can speed oxidation |
| Remake weekly | Freshness is the DIY advantage |
| Patch test | Differin makes surprise irritation more likely |
| Stop if burning persists | Irritation is not the goal |

## 8. Storage and discard rules

Store:

- in the fridge;
- protected from light;
- in a clean bottle;
- ideally make only 7-10 days at a time.

Discard if:

- strong orange/brown color;
- visible contamination;
- odd/rancid smell;
- bottle pressurizes repeatedly;
- it stings much more than usual;
- it has been sitting warm for days.

The Lab Muffin DIY video notes that a simple protected DIY serum may stay usable-looking around one week before yellowing and can be quite yellow by two weeks. Treat that as a practical ceiling, not a safety guarantee.

## 9. Does yellowing tell you how much active vitamin C is left?

**Not reliably.** Color is a useful qualitative warning, not a potency meter. A clear serum can have lost L-ascorbic acid without producing enough colored material to see, while a pale-yellow serum can still contain a meaningful amount of L-ascorbic acid. The color change and the loss of intact L-ascorbic acid do not have one universal, bottle-to-bottle conversion because pH, oxygen, heat, light, metal ions, packaging, concentration, and the other ingredients change both reaction rates and which products accumulate. [[5]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8773188/)

The practical decision rule is:

| Appearance in a simple L-ascorbic-acid/water serum | What it suggests | Practical action |
|---|---|---|
| Clear/colorless | No obvious visible browning; this does **not** prove full potency | Use the age, storage history, and pH as additional evidence |
| Very pale straw-yellow | Early oxidation or a small amount of downstream colored material is plausible; an exact remaining percentage is unknowable | Use soon if the batch is otherwise fresh and comfortable, or replace it if you want a conservative DIY rule |
| Yellow-gold to orange | More extensive oxidation/degradation or accumulation of colored secondary products is likely | Discard a DIY batch rather than trying to rescue it |
| Orange-brown to brown | Later browning chemistry/polymeric color bodies are likely; intact L-ascorbic acid is no longer trustworthy | Discard |
| Pink, green, blue, gray/black, unexpected cloudiness, particles, gas, or a new odor | Not a normal simple aging sequence; could be contamination, metal/ingredient chemistry, or preservation failure | Discard without trying to identify the color at home |

### 9.1 What can form, and what color should you expect?

There is no single exhaustive list for every serum. The products depend on whether the formula is acidic or alkaline, oxygenated or oxygen-limited, warm or cool, and whether it contains amino compounds, sugars, polyphenols, or trace metals. The table below covers the **major and commonly reported pathway families**, not every transient intermediate. Food and analytical studies are useful for identifying chemistry, but their heated solutions are not a quantitative shelf-life model for a cosmetic serum. [[6]](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jf9805404)[[7]](https://academic.oup.com/ijfst/article/54/9/2770/7805484)

| Product or product family | How it fits the pathway | Expected visible appearance in a dilute serum | Does it represent intact vitamin C? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L-ascorbic acid (LAA)** | The intended, reduced form | Fresh aqueous LAA is normally clear/colorless; the raw material or formula may have a faint tint | **Yes** |
| **Monodehydroascorbate / ascorbyl radical** | Short-lived one-electron oxidation intermediate | Not a practical bottle-color marker; it is too transient to identify by eye | Not a stable reservoir to count |
| **Dehydroascorbic acid (DHA)** | The first major two-electron oxidation product; in principle it can be reduced back, but it also hydrolyzes in water | No fixed color rule. Isolated DHA specifications allow colorless through yellow/orange solutions depending on conditions, so “yellow = DHA = X% left” is not valid. [[10]](https://www.sigmaaldrich.com/GB/en/specification-sheet/ALDRICH/261556) | **Not intact LAA**; biological recycling does not guarantee bottle reversion |
| **2,3-Diketogulonic acid (DKG)** | Irreversible hydrolysis/degradation product after DHA; it is a branch point for later products | No distinctive consumer-visible color is established; expect little direct color from the molecule itself | **No** |
| **L-Erythrulose, threosone, xylosone, and 3-deoxythreosone** | Reactive carbonyl fragments reported downstream of DKG/ascorbate breakdown; some can react with amino groups | Usually not useful as individual eye-visible markers; their secondary reactions can produce yellow, red, or brown pigments | **No** |
| **Threonic acid, oxalic acid, oxalyl-threonate, and related small acids** | Further oxidative cleavage products from DKG/DHA | Generally expected to be colorless or too dilute to drive visible color; this is a chemistry-based inference, not a visual assay | **No** |
| **Cyclic oxalyl-threonolactone, oxalyl threonate isomers, and L-threonolactone** | Short-lived or further-oxidized products reported in DHA/DKG chemistry | No practical naked-eye color assignment; analytical products rather than a consumer color marker | **No** |
| **Furfural and 3-deoxy-L-pentosone** | Acid-catalyzed, often oxygen-limited/non-oxidative route; low pH and heat favor this family | Furfural can be colorless-to-amber and darken toward red-brown in air/light, but its later reactions are more relevant to serum browning than the parent molecule itself. [[11]](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/furfural) | **No** |
| **2-Furoic acid** | Reported downstream product of the aerobic acidic route through DHA | Reported as colorless in aqueous degradation work; unlikely to explain strong yellowing by itself | **No** |
| **3-Hydroxy-2-pyrone and related furanones** | Commonly reported acidic degradation products; 3-hydroxy-2-pyrone can track degradation/browning in some model systems. [[12]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22994409/) | No universal finished-serum color assignment; low-level individual molecules may be visually insignificant, while related furanones can be brown | **No** |
| **3,4-Dihydroxy-5-methyl-2(5H)-furanone** | One identified DHA-solution degradation product | Reported as **brown**; a direct example of a colored downstream product rather than “yellow DHA” alone [[8]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4190239/) | **No** |
| **5-(Hydroxymethyl)furfural (HMF), other furans, carbonyls, and small organic acids** | Additional heat-, pH-, oxygen-, and solvent-dependent products; more relevant when the formula contains sugars or is overheated | Many are colorless/low-color at first; their condensation/polymerization can push the solution toward yellow, tan, orange, or brown | **No** |
| **Alkaline-route furans and ketones** | Reported under higher-pH conditions, including 2-methylfuran, 2,4-dimethylfuran, 2-acetyl-5-methylfuran, and 2-methyl-2-cyclopentanone | Usually not assigned a useful consumer color individually; a higher-pH batch can develop a different yellow/brown mixture as secondary products accumulate | **No** |
| **Condensation products, quinonoid/polymeric color bodies, and melanoidin-like pigments** | Later reactions among carbonyl products and, when present, amino acids/peptides or other nucleophiles | Main explanation for progressively deeper yellow, orange, red-brown, or brown color; exact mixture is formula-specific | **No** |

The key distinction is that **DHA is not the same thing as the brown material**. LAA oxidation can begin with a relatively low-color, reversible product, then proceed through hydrolysis and smaller carbonyl compounds. Those compounds can react further and create colored molecules or polymers. In an amino-containing system, ascorbate/DHA degradation products can generate red or yellow intermediates and later brown products; a pure water-and-LAA batch has fewer routes to that kind of pigment. [[8]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4190239/)[[9]](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/bbb1961/31/1/31_1_101/_article)

### 9.2 The main pathways in a pH-3-to-3.5 DIY serum

For the kind of acidic DIY formula described here, the most useful map is:

1. **Oxygen-present route:** LAA → DHA → DKG → smaller acids/carbonyls, with reported products including 2-furoic acid and 3-hydroxy-2-pyrone. [[5]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8773188/)[[6]](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jf9805404)
2. **Oxygen-limited acid/heat route:** LAA can form 3-deoxy-L-pentosone and furfural without requiring DHA as the main intermediate. Furfural can then participate in later browning chemistry. [[6]](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jf9805404)
3. **Higher-pH route:** the ionized ascorbate fraction rises sharply as pH increases, and alkaline degradation produces a different mixture of furans, ketones, and organic acids. This is one reason an inaccurately neutralized or baking-soda-heavy batch can age differently from a measured pH-3.3 batch. [[5]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8773188/)[[7]](https://academic.oup.com/ijfst/article/54/9/2770/7805484)
4. **Metal/light/heat acceleration:** trace copper or iron, oxygen exposure, light, and warmth can accelerate the initial oxidation and subsequent breakdown. The resulting color still cannot be back-calculated to a remaining LAA percentage. [[5]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8773188/)

### 9.3 What would actually measure the active amount?

- A **stability-indicating HPLC/LC method** that separates and quantifies LAA, DHA, and relevant degradation products is the appropriate way to answer “how much LAA is left.”
- A home DCPIP or iodine titration measures reducing capacity and can be confounded by other reducing ingredients; it is not a reliable LAA-versus-DHA assay for a finished serum.
- A consistent photo against a white background, plus recorded batch age, pH, temperature, and first color-change date, can help you set a conservative personal discard window. It cannot establish potency.

So: **pale yellow is not a percentage estimate; orange/brown is a strong discard signal.** If the goal is predictable active LAA, make smaller batches, keep them cold and dark, minimize headspace/air exchange, and replace them before they reach the orange-brown stage.

## 10. What about adding vitamin E and ferulic acid?

You can, but the difficulty jumps:

- vitamin E is oil-soluble;
- ferulic acid is not as easy as dumping powder into water;
- you need solvent/surfactant strategy;
- preservation and stability get more complex;
- the exact SkinCeuticals patent is a solvent/stability system, not just the shopping list.

For home DIY, I would rather make a fresh simple LAA serum than a fake CEF clone that separates or oxidizes unpredictably.

## 11. Fast answer for your current setup

If you have:

- The Ordinary-style dropper bottle;
- L-ascorbic acid powder;
- baking soda;
- no pH strips;
- Differin at night;

Then do this:

1. Make **10% or 15%**, not 20%, for the first no-strip batch.
2. Use the gram table above.
3. Mix LAA in water first, then add baking soda.
4. Keep cap loose while fizzing.
5. Use only in the morning.
6. Refrigerate and remake weekly.
7. If your skin stings for more than a minute or looks irritated, reduce frequency or dilute by applying moisturizer first.
