# Storage, testing, and stop rules

*Compiled 2026-08-15. Research orientation, not medical advice. No shelf life is assigned to an unvalidated home batch.*

## 0. Bottom line

Storage advice depends on whether the failure you are trying to prevent is chemical oxidation, physical separation, or microbial growth. One control rarely solves all three.

## 1. Dry powder storage

| Practice | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Keep the original, labeled container closed | Powders absorb humidity and can pick up contaminants |
| Store cool, dry, dark, and outside the bathroom | Heat/light/moisture accelerate chemical and physical changes |
| Use a clean, dry scoop; never return a touched scoop | Prevents contamination of the bulk jar |
| Record lot and opening date | Lets you trace a problem and detect long-opened raw material |
| Do not freeze by default | Condensation during thawing can wet the bulk powder; follow supplier instructions instead |
| Do not use a powder with unknown identity, missing COA, strong unexpected odor, or visible contamination | A cosmetic label is not an identity or microbial certificate |

“Dry” does not mean sterile. A raw powder can carry microorganisms or contaminants into a water formula.

## 2. Water-based batch storage

For a home-made water product without a validated preservative system, I would use **single-use aliquots and discard the remainder** rather than inventing a 7-day, 30-day, or 3-month shelf life. Refrigeration can slow some chemical reactions and microbial growth, but it does not demonstrate safety. FDA identifies water, raw materials, packaging, and consumer handling as contamination routes and notes that preservative efficacy should be tested under foreseeable storage/use conditions. [[1]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/potential-contaminants-cosmetics/microbiological-safety-and-cosmetics)[[2]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-guidance-documents/good-manufacturing-practice-gmp-guidelinesinspection-checklist-cosmetics)

### If you are evaluating a preserved formula

The minimum serious questions are:

- Is the preservative compatible with the pH, surfactants, polymers, salts, and active ingredients?
- Is the use level within the supplier's specification?
- Is the container compatible and low-touch?
- Has the exact finished formula passed a microbial challenge/preservative efficacy test?
- Has it passed stability at intended and accelerated conditions?

If the answer is “no challenge test,” call the shelf life **unknown**, not “probably fine.”

## 3. Packaging

- **Airless pump:** reduces repeated finger contact and some air exchange; it does not prove sterility.
- **Small squeeze tube:** useful for gels/emulsions when compatible with the formula.
- **Dropper bottle:** convenient but repeatedly exposes the neck and headspace; not ideal for an unpreserved water serum.
- **Open jar:** highest finger-contact burden; poor choice for a water formula.
- **Opaque/amber container:** helps light-sensitive actives such as LAA and kojic acid; does not stop microbial growth.

## 4. What to log

For each batch, record:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Ingredient name and lot | LAA, supplier lot 123 |
| Mass of every component | 1.00 g + 9.00 g |
| Water source | distilled/deionized, date |
| pH | measured after mixing, instrument/strip type |
| Container | opaque pump / single-use vial |
| Date/time made and opened | exact timestamp |
| Storage | dark, room temp or refrigerator |
| Appearance | clear/colorless, yellow, precipitate, separation |
| Skin response | sting duration, redness, next-day condition |

## 5. Patch testing

Patch testing cannot prove that a product is non-irritating everywhere or that it is uncontaminated. It can reduce the chance of a large facial exposure. Test a small area on the forearm or behind the ear with the same batch, wait through the relevant delayed period, and do not escalate if there is persistent redness, swelling, itching, or burning.

For AHAs, FDA recommends following directions and using sun protection; its page summarizes increased UV sensitivity and historical reports of burning, dermatitis, pigmentary changes, and chemical burns, especially with stronger peel-like products. [[3]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/alpha-hydroxy-acids)

## 6. Discard criteria

Discard immediately for:

- mold, film, gas, unexpected bubbles, or visible microbial growth;
- new separation, crystals, grit, or precipitate when the formula should be clear;
- sharp odor change;
- orange/brown darkening of LAA;
- unknown age or unknown storage history;
- a container that has been touched to skin, contaminated, or left open;
- any batch that causes persistent or escalating irritation.

Absence of these signs is not proof of safety or potency.

## Evidence gaps

- Home users rarely have water-quality certificates, microbial enumeration, challenge testing, or HPLC/UPLC potency assays.
- No universal refrigerator storage time can be assigned across ingredients and vehicles.
- Supplier raw-material instructions should override generic internet recipes when they are more restrictive.
