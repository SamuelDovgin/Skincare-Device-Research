# Science, pH, and preservation framework

*Compiled 2026-08-15. Research orientation, not medical advice. Evidence labels: verified / limited / inference / unknown.*

## 0. Bottom line

The correct DIY question is not “what powder has the highest percentage?” It is:

> **Can the ingredient be delivered in a vehicle with a known pH, known concentration, controlled contamination risk, and storage history that still resembles the evidence?**

For a home user, a simple fresh L-ascorbic-acid solution is the clearest candidate because the active and its target pH are well characterized. Glycolic acid has good enough human evidence to justify a finished product or a professionally designed prototype, but its free-acid fraction and irritation risk make powder mixing less forgiving. HA is the reverse: easy to describe, relatively gentle, but not necessarily easy to turn into a clean, reproducible serum.

## 1. What pH actually changes

### L-ascorbic acid

Pure L-ascorbic acid is a water-soluble reducing agent. The classic absorption study found that skin delivery required a formulation below pH 3.5, with maximal tissue saturation around 20% in the tested pig-skin model. That is a delivery finding, not a home-serum shelf-life equation. [[1]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11207686/)

Lower pH can improve the proportion of un-ionized ascorbic acid, but it also increases sting and can be a poor fit for a damaged barrier. Oxygen, light, heat, trace metals, headspace, water quality, viscosity, and co-ingredients change degradation. The archive's [vitamin C topic](../08_vitamin_c_serums/index.html#doc3) and [potency model](../08_vitamin_c_serums/index.html#doc5) should be read for the deeper oxidation pathway; color is only a warning signal, not a potency assay.

### Glycolic acid

Glycolic acid is a small AHA. Its exfoliating activity depends on concentration, pH, vehicle, and the free-acid fraction. FDA states that AHA effects vary with acid type, concentration, pH, and other ingredients; its consumer framework cites ≤10% and final pH ≥3.5, with sun protection. This is a safety/labeling framework, not a guarantee of efficacy. [[2]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/alpha-hydroxy-acids)

The human 5% study used an unneutralized cream, not a jar of powder dissolved in tap water. That difference matters: a cream has a vehicle, manufacturing controls, and a defined pH history. [[3]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9598014/)

### Near-skin-pH ingredients

Niacinamide, sodium hyaluronate, N-acetyl glucosamine, panthenol, urea, sodium PCA, and many peptides do not need a very acidic pH to perform their basic cosmetic role. A pH around 4.5–6.5 is a reasonable educational band for many of these, but the raw material's supplier specification and the finished formula's preservation system control the actual target. This is a formulation inference, not a universal rule.

## 2. Water changes the problem

The moment a powder is dissolved in water, the batch can support microbial growth. FDA lists contaminated water/raw materials, poor manufacturing, ineffective preservation, packaging, shipping/storage, and finger-dipping as contamination routes. FDA also says not to assume that adding water is harmless or that a refrigerator substitutes for preservation. [[4]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/potential-contaminants-cosmetics/microbiological-safety-and-cosmetics)

### Three separate questions

| Question | What it can tell you | What it cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|
| **pH** | Whether the batch is in the intended acid/base range | Whether it is preserved or potent |
| **Color/odor/clarity** | A visible deterioration warning | Whether pathogens are absent or how much active remains |
| **Preservative added** | That a preservation strategy exists | That the system works without challenge testing |

FDA's cosmetic GMP checklist expects testing of raw materials, water, in-process and finished products, plus preservation/challenge testing under reasonably foreseeable use. A home batch does not meet that evidentiary bar merely because it contains a commercial preservative. [[5]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-guidance-documents/good-manufacturing-practice-gmp-guidelinesinspection-checklist-cosmetics)

## 3. Stability is not one thing

| Stability layer | Typical DIY failure | Best control |
|---|---|---|
| **Chemical** | LAA oxidizes; kojic acid or botanical extracts darken; urea changes with heat/pH | correct pH, low oxygen, light protection, cool storage, compatible ingredients |
| **Physical** | HA clumps; azelaic/salicylic acid crystals settle; oil and water separate | correct solvent/vehicle, mixing order, viscosity control, suspension/emulsion design |
| **Microbial** | bacteria, yeast, or mold grow invisibly in a water serum | validated preservative system, hygienic process, packaging, challenge testing |
| **Dose** | a tiny amount of powder is inaccurately weighed; bulk powder absorbs water | scale resolution, mass-based calculations, sealed dry storage |

Chemical stability and microbial safety can move in opposite directions. A low pH may slow some organisms and improve LAA delivery, but it does not make a contaminated product safe; some acid-tolerant organisms can still grow. Conversely, an ingredient can be chemically stable near pH 5–6 while the water product is microbiologically vulnerable.

## 4. Why high pH is not the answer

High pH may increase irritation, alter the ionization and degradation pathway of LAA, and move a leave-on formula away from the skin's normal acid mantle. It is sometimes used in a deliberate neutralization/buffering system, but “add baking soda until it feels less stingy” is not a controlled formulation method. The desired pH must be measured, and the acid/base balance must be calculated by mass and verified after full equilibration.

## 5. A practical evidence ladder

1. **Human topical outcome at a stated concentration and vehicle.** Strongest for efficacy.
2. **Human tolerability or barrier endpoint.** Useful, but not equivalent to pigment or wrinkle efficacy.
3. **Ex vivo / in vitro transport or mechanism.** Explains plausibility; does not prove a home result.
4. **Supplier technical sheet.** Useful for solubility, pH, and handling; not clinical proof.
5. **Marketplace recipe or social post.** A hypothesis only.

The explorer and audit use this ladder. A high efficacy rating never overrides a poor formulation or preservation rating.

## Evidence gaps

- No source found provides a validated shelf-life equation for a home-prepared mixed powder serum.
- Finished-product evidence often combines several ingredients, making the individual contribution uncertain.
- “Cosmetic grade” is not one universal quality specification; request a lot-specific COA and microbial/heavy-metal limits.
- The ideal pH for a home formula depends on the active, vehicle, preservative, and skin tolerance; it cannot be inferred from the word “acid.”
