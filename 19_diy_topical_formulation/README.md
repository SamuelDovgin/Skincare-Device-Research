# DIY Topical Formulation: Powder Ingredients, pH, Stability, and Storage

*Compiled 2026-08-15. Research orientation, not medical advice. This topic is for learning and small-scale formulation decisions; it is not a manufacturing or treatment protocol. Confidence labels: **verified** = primary/regulatory or human clinical source; **limited** = small, product-specific, or mechanistic evidence; **inference** = formulation interpretation; **unknown** = the needed home-batch data do not exist.*

## Scope

This topic audits common cosmetic powder actives that a home user might buy online and dissolve, suspend, or disperse into a facial product. It also now covers a separate electrolyzed-water lane for hypochlorous acid (HOCl): how to scale a salt-water starting batch, measure FAC and pH, calibrate a generator, decide whether a pillowcase experiment is worth attempting, and keep DIY generation separate from future facial use. It connects the archive's existing [vitamin C serum lane](../08_vitamin_c_serums/index.html) and [glycolic-acid lane](../18_glycolic_acid_topicals/index.html), but adds the missing decision surface: **can a home mixer make a reproducible, stable, appropriately preserved product from this powder or salt-water process?**

It covers efficacy, solubility, pH, oxidation, water activity, contamination, packaging, storage, and safer alternatives. It excludes sterile preparations, injections, microneedling cargo, prescription-drug compounding, sunscreen manufacturing, pigments/clays as a category, and a literal inventory of every chemical sold as a powder.

## Bottom line

1. **Best first DIY candidate:** a small, simple L-ascorbic-acid test batch if you can weigh by mass, measure pH, use cosmetic/USP-grade raw material, and treat it as a fresh batch rather than a shelf-stable serum. The existing vitamin C topic's low-pH evidence supports this narrow use case; it does not validate a long-opened bottle. [[1]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11207686/)
2. **Glycolic acid is effective enough to study but harder to DIY well.** A 5% unneutralized cream improved texture and discoloration in a randomized 3-month study, while FDA's consumer framework emphasizes ≤10% and final pH ≥3.5. Powder-in-water without a calibrated pH meter, buffering plan, preservative system, and patch-test discipline is a poor shortcut. [[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9598014/)[[3]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/alpha-hydroxy-acids)
3. **Hyaluronic acid is a weak DIY candidate despite being “gentle.”** It can hydrate skin, but raw powder varies by molecular weight, clumps easily, needs very low use levels, and turns a water batch into a microbial-preservation problem. A finished, preserved serum is usually the better learning and skin-care choice. [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38829483/)
4. **Niacinamide, N-acetyl glucosamine, tranexamic acid, and urea are formulation-friendly powders, but the water formula still needs preservation.** Ingredient ease is not product safety. FDA identifies contaminated water, raw materials, packaging, and finger-dipping as common contamination routes. [[5]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/potential-contaminants-cosmetics/microbiological-safety-and-cosmetics)
5. **Poor DIY candidates:** salicylic acid, azelaic acid, ferulic acid, resveratrol, kojic acid, alpha-lipoic acid, peptide powders, copper peptides, and botanical extracts. Their solubility, oxidation, pH, delivery, or dosing problems are not solved by adding more powder.
6. **There is no universal “best pH.”** Low pH helps L-ascorbic-acid delivery and increases the free-acid fraction of glycolic acid, but can sting and damage the barrier. Most humectants and barrier ingredients belong nearer skin pH. High pH is not a good default for leave-on facial products.
7. **Refrigeration slows some chemical reactions; it does not preserve an unpreserved water product.** A fridge is not a microbial challenge test, and color, smell, or clarity cannot prove the absence of contamination or confirm active potency.
8. **Hypochlorous acid water is a measurement problem, not a recipe-only problem.** A useful home calibration target is roughly 150–250 ppm FAC with final pH 5.0–6.5, preferably around 180–220 ppm and pH 5.5–6.2. The planner treats that as a project QC window—not a safety certification—and keeps DIY fabric experiments separate from face-labeled commercial sprays.

## Recommended learning path

1. Start with the [candidate explorer](diy_candidate_explorer.html) to see the difference between ingredient efficacy and DIY suitability.
2. If your question is HOCl water, open the [Hypochlorous water planner](hypochlorous_water_planner.html) first, then read [Hypochlorous acid water](index.html#doc6) before buying a generator or measuring a batch.
3. If you are evaluating the L-ascorbic-acid worksheet, open the [Vitamin C recipe scaler](vitamin_c_recipe_scaler.html) and change one input at a time.
4. Read the [science and preservation framework](index.html#doc1).
5. Use the [ingredient audit](index.html#doc2) to shortlist one powder, not a stack.
6. Read the [prototype worksheets](index.html#doc3) before mixing anything.
7. Finish with [storage and stop rules](index.html#doc4), especially if the formula contains water.
8. Before using any marketplace powder, run the [raw-material COA checker](raw_material_coa_checker.html) and retain the exact lot document, invoice, and package photos.

## Status and open items

- The candidate matrix is a transparent evidence-and-formulation heuristic, not a regulatory approval list or microbial safety certificate.
- No home-batch shelf life is assigned. A real shelf-life claim requires stability testing, preservative efficacy/challenge testing, and batch controls that a home kitchen normally does not have. [[6]](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-guidance-documents/good-manufacturing-practice-gmp-guidelinesinspection-checklist-cosmetics)
- Supplier-specific certificates of analysis, particle size, molecular weight, residual solvents, heavy metals, microbial limits, and identity testing remain unresolved for marketplace powders.
- The new COA gate makes those missing fields explicit, but home users still cannot qualify a supplier or reproduce laboratory identity/contaminant testing.
- The glycolic-acid worksheet intentionally stops short of a “pour powder into water and store it” recipe because acid equivalent, final pH, buffering, and preservation are coupled variables.
- The HOCl page intentionally treats DIY water as a measured fabric/hygiene experiment rather than a facial product. Current product pages were checked 2026-08-22; prices, stock, labels, and formulas should be rechecked before purchase.
