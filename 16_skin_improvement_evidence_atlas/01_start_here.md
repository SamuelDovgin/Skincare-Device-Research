# Start here: the evidence-weighted skin-improvement map

*Compiled 2026-08-02. The atlas is a research orientation tool, not a diagnosis or treatment prescription.*

## 0. Bottom line

There is no single “best skin-improvement technology.” The most reliable choice is the intervention whose demonstrated endpoint matches the user’s problem and whose delivery parameters are actually disclosed. The map therefore weights **human evidence volume, study-design strength, endpoint fit, and transparency**; it does not use market size, social popularity, or a dramatic mechanism as a proxy for efficacy.

## 1. How to read the visual

- **Node area:** evidence score derived from the mapped corpus. Bigger means more research support is available to inspect.
- **Color:** primary goal family — pigment, acne/barrier, texture/scars, laxity, hair, or systemic/emerging.
- **Badge:** confidence tier. “Established” means repeat human evidence and/or high-level synthesis for a defined endpoint; it does not mean every product in the category works.
- **Home translation:** high, mixed, or low. This is a separate field because a clinic study and a consumer device are not interchangeable.
- **Click:** opens the category’s best-supported use, evidence count, caveat, and the existing deep-dive route.

## 2. The five-minute reading path

1. Open the [interactive map](evidence_atlas.html) and select your goal.
2. Click the two largest nodes with the best endpoint fit, then read their evidence rows.
3. Read the [goal matrix](04_goal_to_intervention_matrix.md) for the practical “start / consider / skip” logic.
4. Open the linked deep-dive topic for device specs, regulatory documents, exact protocols, and product-specific gaps.
5. Before combining anything, read the [safety gates](05_actionable_protocols_and_safety.md).

## 3. Executive evidence signals

| Goal | Highest-confidence first move | Where devices fit | Main trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pigment, melasma, PIH | Daily broad-spectrum photoprotection; visible-light protection matters when pigment is the target; add condition-matched topical therapy | Lasers/IPL can help selected patients but raise recurrence/PIH and parameter questions | Treating “brightening” as one endpoint and ignoring skin type, relapse, or irritation |
| Acne | Proven acne actives plus tolerable barrier support | Blue/red LED and selected lasers are adjuncts; 1726 nm is promising but not a generic home-device claim | Replacing evidence-based acne treatment with microbiome or light marketing |
| Texture / photoaging | Retinoid-compatible routine and, when desired, a studied remodeling procedure | Fractional laser and microneedling have meaningful human evidence; home devices are lower-dose translations | Comparing watts, mJ, or “collagen” slogans without coverage, depth, or follow-up |
| Laxity | Match the problem: skin quality, mild laxity, or volume loss | RF/MFU can improve selected laxity endpoints; injectables and surgery address different anatomy | Calling heat, collagen stimulation, and a lift equivalent |
| Hair removal | Follicle-targeted laser/IPL with skin-type and hair-color matching | Home IPL/diode can work for selected users but needs maintenance | Using IPL marketed for hair as a pigment or rejuvenation device |
| Barrier / recovery | Gentle cleanser, moisturizer/lipids, and photoprotection | Light or devices are secondary unless a specific indication is established | Stacking acids, retinoids, heat, needles, and occlusion during recovery |

## 4. What “more research” does not mean

Evidence volume increases confidence in a **category-endpoint pair**, not in every device, formula, or seller. The map cannot infer that a $60 OEM mask equals a sham-controlled clinical mask, that a peptide blend contains the studied concentration, or that a regulatory predicate proves a clinical outcome. Those remain product-level questions.
