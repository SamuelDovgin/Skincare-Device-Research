# Pigment, photoaging, sunscreen, and skin of color

*Compiled 2026-07-14. Pigment disorders can be medical conditions; this evidence map is not a diagnosis or a self-treatment protocol.*

## 0. Bottom line

Recent pigment research converges on **photoprotection first, irritation control second, active selection third**. For melasma and PIH, visible light and UVA matter in addition to UVB. Tinted/iron-oxide sunscreens have a plausible and increasingly clinical role, especially for darker phototypes, but the field still lacks a universal visible-light rating and has inadequate shade diversity. New depigmenting actives are interesting, but most have much smaller and less independent evidence than hydroquinone, retinoids, or broad-spectrum sunscreen.

## 1. Study map

| Study | Design / intervention | What it found | Evidence caution |

| Alpha-arbutin 5% + kojic acid 2% vs triple combination ([PMID 39555866](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39555866/)) | 2025 split-face randomized pilot, n=30, 12 weeks + follow-up | Compared a cosmetic combination with triple-combination cream for melasma | Small pilot; cosmetic formula is not equivalent to isolated ingredients |
| Cysteamine 5% + ectoine vs hydroquinone 4% + ectoine ([PMID 40127492](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40127492/)) | 2025 double-blind randomized trial | Both improved mMASI and quality of life; between-group difference was not significant | Useful comparative signal, but treatment occurred in Indonesia and needs replication |
| Cordyceps cosmetic + oral TXA ([PMID 40590148](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40590148/)) | 2025 clinical trial in women with melasma | Both oral TXA combinations improved melasma | Oral TXA is a medical exposure; the cosmetic cannot be isolated from the co-intervention |
| Oral vs topical TXA ([PMID 40923777](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40923777/)) | 2025 randomized trial; 50 patients; oral TXA 250 mg twice daily vs topical TXA 5% | Directly compares routes over 12 weeks | Systemic TXA requires clinician screening; topical results do not imply oral safety |
| Tinted vs untinted sunscreen ([PMID 41014037](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41014037/)) | 2025 prospective randomized investigator-blinded summer study | Tests visible-light-protective tint for melasma relapse protection | Product and season-specific; adherence and shade matching still matter |
| PTPD-12 cream ([PMID 41044809](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41044809/)) | 2025 randomized split-face study, n=21, 8 weeks | Reduced melanin index versus control side | Very small and short; novel ingredient with limited replication |
| Adapalene 0.1% for photoaging ([PMID 40990960](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40990960/)) | 2025 randomized trial; 58 completers; 6 months | Improved skin-aging score, wrinkles, and pigmentation; stinging/xerosis common early | Small single-center trial; DPAS difference was not significant |
| PIH prevention in skin of color ([PMID 39953770](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39953770/)) | 2025 systematic review; 14 studies/369 cases | Sunscreen was the only consistently successful prevention measure | All participants in included reports were Asian; prevention evidence remains thin |
| Visible-light tinted sunscreen review ([PMID 40552645](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40552645/)) | 2025 review; 67 articles selected from 89 | Tinted products generally outperform untinted products for visible-light/pigment endpoints | No standardized visible-light test or universal shade coverage |
| Oral supplements and photoprotection ([PMID 39804624](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39804624/)) | 2025 systematic review | Human and preclinical supplement signals exist | Supplements are adjuncts, not sunscreen replacements; endpoints and products vary |
| Sclareolide + niacinamide sunscreen ([PMID 41240206](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41240206/)) | 2026 investigator-blinded randomized trial in skin of color | Tests a broad-spectrum formula against PIH induced by combined UV/visible exposure | Product-linked trial; inspect exact comparator and population before generalizing |
| Malassezin 0.75% vs hydroquinone 4% ([PMID 41493251](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41493251/)) | 2026 split-face double-blind randomized trial, n=20 | Directly tests a novel topical against hydroquinone | Small; novelty does not equal superiority or long-term safety |
| Global consensus on melanin hyperpigmentation disorders ([PMID 41362125](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41362125/)) | 2026 international consensus | Sets tailored algorithms by phototype, disease, and access | Expert consensus; not a controlled efficacy comparison |
| Visible-light photoprotection consensus ([PMID 42101389](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42101389/)) | 2026 modified Delphi, 27 statements | Calls for standardized exposure and measurement methods | Establishes a research framework, not a product ranking |
| Tinted inorganic sunscreen with antioxidants ([PMID 42310896](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42310896/)) | 2026 laboratory/photobiology product study | Formula reduced some visible-light/UVA1 erythema and pigmentation endpoints versus ZnO-only comparator | Manufacturer-linked and exposure-model specific |
| Picosecond Alexandrite vs QS/long-pulse Nd:YAG ([PMID 39843829](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39843829/)) | 2025 randomized evaluator-blinded melasma trial | Compares two laser strategies and safety | Procedure-specific; laser outcomes do not displace photoprotection |
| Low-fluence QS Nd:YAG for melasma ([PMID 40932145](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40932145/)) | 2026 blind randomized placebo-controlled trial | Tests low-fluence 1064-nm laser against placebo | Laser plus aftercare and relapse risk must be considered; do not generalize to home lasers |

## 2. What the evidence means for the archive

### Visible light

The shift from “SPF only” to visible-light-aware photoprotection is real, but three separate questions must stay separate:

1. Does visible light worsen pigmentation in a given person or condition?
2. Does a specific tinted formula reduce a measured pigment endpoint after a defined exposure?
3. Will a person apply enough of a shade-matched product every day?

The 2025 review and 2026 consensus support the first two in principle and emphasize that measurement is not standardized. The practical archive conclusion is therefore **use a cosmetically acceptable tinted/iron-oxide sunscreen when pigment is the primary goal**, not “every tint is proven equal.”

### Novel pigment actives

The 2025/2026 trials for PTPD-12, malassezin, cordyceps, cysteamine/ectoine, and arbutin/kojic acid are useful candidate studies. They are not a reason to abandon a stable, tolerable regimen for a new serum. The key missing evidence is independent replication, larger samples, longer recurrence follow-up, and representation across phototypes.

### Retinoids and irritation

The 2025 adapalene trial is particularly relevant to the archive because it provides a recent human photoaging signal for a known acne retinoid, while also documenting stinging and xerosis. It supports the existing `13_combined_skincare_regimen` principle: retinoids can be effective, but a routine must preserve tolerability and photoprotection.

### Procedures and PIH

The PIH systematic review found sunscreen to be the most consistently successful prevention measure and reported that cooling-air devices were associated with worse PIH in the included reports. That does not prove cooling is intrinsically harmful; it highlights confounding and the need to treat post-procedure pigment prevention as a whole protocol rather than a single gadget feature.

## 3. Practical hierarchy

1. Diagnose or confirm the pigment condition when it is persistent, changing, symptomatic, or atypical.
2. Use broad-spectrum sunscreen; for melasma/PIH, favor a well-tolerated tinted/iron-oxide product when practical.
3. Add one evidence-based active at a time and monitor irritation.
4. Treat oral TXA, lasers, peels, and energy devices as clinician-guided options with relapse and PIH planning.
5. Treat novel cosmetic actives as experiments with shorter evidence, not as “natural hydroquinone replacements” by default.

## Evidence gaps

- The apparent visible-light benefit may vary by skin phototype, melasma subtype, pigment depth, exposure spectrum, and formula tint density.
- Included PIH prevention studies were not demographically broad enough to support confident Fitzpatrick V–VI conclusions.
- Many cosmetic trials have small samples and manufacturer involvement.
- Recurrence after stopping treatment is underreported.
- Home IPL and laser devices should not be inferred to treat melasma or PIH from clinic laser studies.
