# Fractional-laser clinical evidence map

*Rapid evidence map updated 2026-08-23. It is not a systematic review or treatment recommendation. Device, wavelength, pulse geometry, coverage, operator, indication, and skin type are not interchangeable.*

## Bottom line

The archive already maps fractional-laser physics, devices, suppliers, patents, and the 1440/1450/1927 nm “quantified gap.” The most important missing piece was a study-level map that answers: **what human outcome is supported, in whom, for how long, and how directly does it transfer to a home device?**

- **Tria’s home 1440 nm lane has direct device-specific human evidence, but it is open-label/baseline-controlled and does not establish superiority to sham or skincare alone.**
- **Professional 1927 nm studies support short-term pigment/photodamage signals**, including in darker skin under low-energy/low-density protocols, but those clinic protocols are not transferable dose recipes for a home device.
- **Durability is the major unresolved endpoint.** Several studies peak at one month, regress by three months, or document melasma recurrence/delayed flaring.
- The strongest decision rule is therefore not “which wavelength won?” It is **exact device + indication + study design + skin type + follow-up**.

## Evidence ladder used here

| Tier | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A** | Randomized controlled comparison that isolates a clinically relevant question |
| **B** | Prospective device-specific human study with blinded/objective assessment but no untreated/sham control |
| **C** | Prospective uncontrolled, retrospective, abstract/poster, or indirect professional analog |
| **D** | Bench, regulatory equivalence, manufacturer report, or mechanism only |

Tier is study-design confidence, not a claim that every Tier A protocol is relevant to this archive’s devices.

## 1. Direct home-device evidence

| Device / source | Design and population | Protocol | Main result | Limitation / tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tria FAN / Age-Defying, FDA K130459** | Prospective open-label baseline comparison; 60 participants, age 32–70 | Home treatment 5 days/week for 12 weeks | 45/60 (75%) had periorbital improvement four weeks after treatment; 37/60 (62%) at 12 weeks after treatment | No untreated/sham control; five participants had skin darkening lasting >3 months. **Tier B/C** [[1]](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf13/K130459.pdf) |
| **Tria company clinical abstract/poster** | 90 enrolled, 87 completed; randomized to low/medium/high settings, blinded graders | Weekday treatments for 12 weeks | Significant changes reported for periorbital wrinkles, dyschromia, and roughness; burning commonly resolved within hours | Setting comparison, not a no-treatment control; company document and publication status unclear. **Tier C** |
| **Tria current owner manual** | Manufacturer synthesis of an 87-person study | Five sessions/week for 12 weeks at the highest comfortable level | Reports 89% smoother-feeling skin and 78% line-reduction self-report | Self-report and manufacturer summary; not an independent effect estimate. **Tier D** |

The FDA file is the most decision-relevant home source because it preserves denominators, follow-up, and the persistent-darkening signal. It supports a modest wrinkle indication for the studied device/protocol; it does not validate generic “fractional laser” rejuvenation or improvised facial pigment treatment.

## 2. Professional 1927 nm pigment and photodamage evidence

| Study | Design / sample | What it found | What it does **not** prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1927 nm + topical tranexamic acid vs saline | Split-face randomized trial, n=46, Fitzpatrick III–V | Both sides improved; no significant TXA advantage at three months | No untreated face, so it does not isolate the laser effect or support home dosing. **Tier A for the adjuvant question** [[2]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32506227/) |
| PIH in skin of color | Retrospective series, n=61, Fitzpatrick IV–VI | Mean investigator improvement 43.24%; no adverse events observed in the report | No randomized comparator; low-energy/low-density clinic protocol is operator- and device-specific. **Tier C** [[3]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31663147/) |
| Facial photodamage | Prospective study, n=27 | Improvement at one month; pigment measures drifted toward baseline by three months | Small uncontrolled cohort; durability is limited. **Tier C** [[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36950878/) |
| Facial photopigmentation | Prospective study, n=40, Fitzpatrick I–IV, two treatments | Moderate-or-greater overall improvement reported in 82% at one month and 69% at three months | No untreated control; response attenuated over follow-up. **Tier C** [[5]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25607696/) |
| Melasma | Prospective case series, n=20 | Initial improvement; recurrence in 7/15 evaluable patients and PIH in 2/20 | Does not support routine melasma treatment, especially without diagnosis and long follow-up. **Tier C** [[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23255085/) |
| Delayed melasma flare/unmasking | Retrospective report, 13 cases, Fitzpatrick III–IV | Describes flaring or new recognition after 1927 nm treatment, often delayed | Cannot estimate incidence, but defeats the assumption that short follow-up captures pigment risk. **Safety signal** [[7]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42460183/) |

### Interpretation

For discrete photopigmentation, professional 1927 nm has a plausible short-term clinical signal. For melasma or pigment-prone skin, the evidence is more conditional: low-density protocols may be tolerated, yet recurrence, PIH, and delayed flaring remain real uncertainty. A wavelength label alone cannot resolve that tradeoff.

## 3. Combined 1440 + 1927 nm evidence

A 2024 prospective study of the Clear + Brilliant Touch platform enrolled 28 participants and used four monthly combined-wavelength sessions. Multiple rejuvenation outcomes improved, but the study was uncontrolled. This supports a device-class signal, not a head-to-head claim that combined wavelengths outperform either wavelength alone or a home 1440 nm unit. **Tier C.** [[8]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39190540/)

The archive’s [1440/1450 vs 1927 head-to-head](index.html#doc9) remains the right physics comparison; this page adds the missing clinical-design boundary.

## 4. Decision matrix

| Goal | Best-supported lane in this map | Confidence | Decision implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mild periorbital wrinkles with a studied Tria home device | Direct K130459 home study | **Low–moderate** | Reasonable only within exact-model IFU; expect modest, variable change, not clinic resurfacing |
| Diffuse photopigmentation | Professional 1927 nm clinician protocols | **Moderate signal, low transferability** | Diagnosis, skin type, density, and operator matter more than buying a wavelength-matched home device |
| Melasma | No simple green-light lane | **Uncertain / relapse-prone** | Do not treat a melasma label as equivalent to lentigines; recurrence and PIH need explicit discussion |
| Texture/roughness | Home Tria and professional combination cohorts show signals | **Low–moderate** | Standardized photos and durability follow-up are needed; subjective smoothness alone is weak |
| “Deep collagen remodeling” or clinic-equivalent results | Not established for current home devices | **Low** | Do not infer depth or effect size from wavelength or discomfort |

## 5. Minimum outcome set for future studies

The next useful trial is not another before/after satisfaction survey. It should report:

1. exact model, wavelength, pulse energy/duration, spot, coverage/density, passes, cadence, and skin cooling;
2. randomized sham or untreated split-face control where feasible;
3. Fitzpatrick type and pigment diagnosis, analyzed by subgroup;
4. blinded validated wrinkle/pigment grading plus objective imaging;
5. denominators, withdrawals, adverse events, and persistent pigment changes;
6. follow-up at one, three, six, and twelve months after stopping treatment;
7. prespecified minimal clinically important change—not statistical significance alone.

## Evidence gaps

- No credible randomized head-to-head compares the studied home 1440 nm Tria with professional 1927 nm at matched endpoints.
- Home-study reports do not provide a clean sham-controlled estimate of benefit.
- Professional settings cannot be converted into home fluence or coverage instructions.
- Darker skin types and melasma-prone populations remain underrepresented in direct home-device evidence.
- Long-term durability, retreatment frequency, and cumulative pigment risk are sparsely measured.
- Several device-specific sources are regulatory or company documents rather than full peer-reviewed trials.
