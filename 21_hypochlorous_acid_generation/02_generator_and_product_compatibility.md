# Generator and Product Compatibility: What Must Be True Before Calibration

## Compatibility is a gate, not a score

“Compatible” in this topic means only: *the documented generator/setup has enough evidence to enter the generic acidified-brine calibration workflow*. It does not mean a resulting batch is compatible with a person, a wound, the face, eyes, breathing zone, bedding, a device surface, or a regulatory use claim.

The [generator & product-compatibility checker](product_compatibility_checker.html) asks five questions in order. A single failed hard gate blocks the generic recipe.

| Gate | Compatible outcome | Incompatible or incomplete outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Manual chemistry | Manual explicitly permits salt + 5% white vinegar **before electrolysis** | Salt-only, vinegar not mentioned, capsule-only, unknown, or manual calls for a different chemistry |
| Device/reservoir material | Manual says its non-metal path is compatible | Metal contact, unidentified materials, damaged/corroded parts, or a manual warning against the formulation |
| FAC method | Fresh method can read the expected high range without saturation | 0–10 ppm pool strip, absent strip chart, expired/unknown method, or no FAC test |
| pH method | Calibrated meter or a sufficiently narrow pH method can distinguish the target band | No pH test or a broad method that cannot distinguish 5–6.5 from the surrounding bands |
| Intended path | Fresh-batch calibration record / non-contact research record | Face/skin, eyes, aerosol/air, or face-contact bedding is selected as an approved DIY route |

## Why this distinguishes product identity from chemistry

Two devices can both be called “HOCl generators” but use different electrodes, reservoirs, salt amounts, intended concentration ranges, control logic, and validation. The generic source formula applies only to the portable unit actually described in the publication. A maker’s manual can be stricter, and the maker owns warranty and material compatibility.

CDC notes that chlorine solutions can be corrosive to metals and that acid mixed with hypochlorite can release toxic chlorine gas. Those are enough reasons to reject a metal/unknown setup and to stop when the manual does not explicitly support the acidified workflow. [[1]](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/disinfection-sterilization/chemical-disinfectants.html)

## Specific PWPAM result

For the supplied PWPAM manual, choose **“salt + water only / sodium-hypochlorite cleaning unit”** in the checker. The result should be **not compatible with this vinegar-based HOCl calibration workflow**. That does not say the device is broken; it keeps its salt-only cleaning documentation separate from a different source’s acidified-brine experiment. [[2]](../19_diy_topical_formulation/source_docs/pwpam_manual_2026-08-24_recipe-and-use.jpg)

## Reading an “incomplete” result

An incomplete result is not permission to continue. It means one of the information gates is missing. The next step is to find the manual language, identify reservoir/electrode materials, obtain a suitable FAC method and pH method, or choose a finished appropriately labeled product instead. It is never “add a little more vinegar and see.”

## What the checker deliberately does not judge

- It does not compare commercial finished HOCl sprays by efficacy or price.
- It does not predict actual pH, FAC, HOCl fraction, storage stability, or material corrosion.
- It does not issue a facial, health, surface-disinfection, or legal label claim.
- It does not replace a manufacturer’s instructions or an SDS.
